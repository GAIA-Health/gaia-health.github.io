Ovia vs What to Expect vs BabyCenter: 2026 Guide

These three come up together in almost every "best pregnancy app" search, but they're not doing the same job. Ovia is a clinical tracker, now consolidated into the Labcorp-owned Ovia Cycle & Pregnancy Tracker. What to Expect is built for week-by-week editorial content. BabyCenter leans on free community and 3D baby visuals. What's easy to miss: What to Expect and BabyCenter are owned by the same company. Here's what each actually does, and who owns your data while they do it.

The Real Difference: Tracker, Content, and Community

Ovia tracks. It's built around the practical mechanics of pregnancy: kick counts, contraction timing, a food safety lookup you can search by ingredient, and symptom logging. Since March 2026, its cycle and pregnancy tracking live in one consolidated app rather than separate apps for fertility and pregnancy, and that app is owned by Labcorp, the clinical lab company.

What to Expect informs. Its strength is depth of content: week-by-week guides built on the bestselling "What to Expect" book series, expert-reviewed articles, and a long-running Moms Forum. It doesn't have a kick counter or contraction timer, and it isn't trying to be a tracking tool.

BabyCenter connects. Its Birth Club groups sort members by due-date month, so you're talking to people at your exact stage, and its 3D fetal development videos are a genuine draw for visual learners. It's completely free with no paywall, which is a meaningful difference from the other two.

For raw tracking tools, Ovia is the closer comparison to a dedicated health app. For content and community, What to Expect and BabyCenter are the closer comparison to each other, and that comparison gets more interesting once you know they share a parent company.

Ovia and the March 2026 Consolidation

Ovia has been owned by Labcorp since 2021. In March 2026, Labcorp folded the separate Ovia Fertility, Ovia Pregnancy, and Ovia Parenting apps into a single app, the Ovia Cycle & Pregnancy Tracker. If you used the standalone Ovia Pregnancy app before, your account and history moved into the consolidated app rather than disappearing.

Pros

  • Kick counter and contraction timer both included, not locked behind a separate purchase
  • Food safety lookup tool: search a specific food to check if it's considered safe during pregnancy
  • Cycle history carries over if you used Ovia for fertility or TTC tracking first
  • Often available at no direct cost through an employer or health-plan benefit

Cons

  • No standalone free tier. Pricing runs roughly $150/year or $22/month if you're not on an employer or health-plan version — verify the current price in-app, since this figure isn't consistently published
  • Owned by Labcorp. Employer-sponsored versions can share de-identified health data with the employer or insurer providing the benefit
  • Community features are smaller and less active than What to Expect's or BabyCenter's
  • No 3D visuals, no wearable integration, no nutrition tracking beyond the food safety lookup

Download: Available on iOS and Android

What to Expect: Content First

What to Expect's whole identity is editorial depth. It's built on the bestselling "What to Expect When You're Expecting" book series, and that content pedigree shows in the app: clear, thorough, medically reviewed articles for nearly every week and every question a first-time parent tends to have.

Pros

  • Week-by-week content is among the most thorough available, and it's free
  • Moms Forum gives you a Q&A community without needing a second app
  • Book-brand recognition means many providers are already familiar with its framing
  • Post-delivery Baby Tracker continues into feeding, pumping, and diapers so you don't switch apps right after birth

Cons

  • Sells personal data, including name, location, due date, and health survey responses, to third parties for targeted advertising
  • Ad-supported, which some users find distracting inside an already information-dense app
  • No kick counter or contraction timer
  • Symptom tracking and nutrition tracking are both basic to nonexistent

Download: Available on iOS and Android

BabyCenter: Free Community and 3D Visuals

BabyCenter's draw is that everything is free, and its Birth Club groups genuinely work: being grouped with people due the same month makes the community feel relevant rather than generic. Its 3D fetal development videos are also a step up visually from the themed size comparisons other content apps use.

Pros

  • Completely free, with no premium tier or paywall on any feature
  • Birth Club communities organized by due date month, widely described as the most active groups of any pregnancy app
  • 3D fetal development videos, plus a kick counter and free childbirth classes
  • Handles pregnancy loss with more care in its content than most competitors

Cons

  • Reporting has raised significant data-sharing concerns. BabyCenter collects a wide range of data types and shares them with third parties for advertising, and it's been named in reporting on data broker practices
  • Owned by Everyday Health, the same parent company as What to Expect
  • No contraction timer, no nutrition tracking, no wearable integration
  • Symptom tracking is basic compared to a dedicated health tracker

Download: Available on iOS and Android

Who Owns Your Data: A Side-by-Side

Pregnancy data is some of the most sensitive information you'll put in any app, so it's worth looking at the business model behind each one rather than just the feature list.

App Parent company How the business is funded What that means for your data
Ovia Labcorp Subscription (~$150/yr standalone) or employer/health-plan sponsorship On employer-sponsored versions, de-identified health data can be shared with the employer or insurer funding the benefit. Standalone subscribers aren't part of that arrangement.
What to Expect Everyday Health Free, ad-supported Sells personal data, including name, location, due date, and health survey responses, to third parties for targeted advertising.
BabyCenter Everyday Health Free, ad-supported Sells personal data to third parties for advertising. Named in reporting on data broker practices tied to pregnancy and parenting apps.

The detail worth sitting with: What to Expect and BabyCenter aren't really two independent choices when it comes to privacy. They're two apps built by the same company on the same ad-funded model, and both have disclosed selling data. Ovia's tradeoff is different: no ads, but employer or health-plan sponsorship can route de-identified data back to whoever's paying for your access. None of these is inherently "safe" or "unsafe." Read the current privacy policy for whichever app you're using, especially if it's an employer-provided version.

Which Should You Choose?

There's no single "best" app here. It depends on what you need most:

Choose Ovia if:

  • You want a kick counter, contraction timer, and food safety lookup in one place
  • You have access through an employer or health-plan benefit
  • You used Ovia for fertility tracking and want your history to carry over

Choose What to Expect if:

  • You're a first-time parent who wants detailed, trustworthy editorial content
  • You want a free app with a well-established Moms Forum
  • You're comfortable with an ad-supported, data-sharing business model

  • You want everything completely free with no paywall

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the real difference between Ovia, What to Expect, and BabyCenter?

Ovia is a clinical tracking app: kick counter, contraction timer, food safety lookup, and a consolidated cycle-to-pregnancy history, now called the Ovia Cycle & Pregnancy Tracker and owned by Labcorp. What to Expect is an editorial app built on the bestselling book series, strongest for week-by-week content and a Moms Forum. BabyCenter is a free community app built around Birth Club groups organized by due date, plus 3D fetal development visuals. All three can log a pregnancy, but they're built for different jobs: tracking, reading, and connecting.

Are What to Expect and BabyCenter really owned by the same company?

Both What to Expect and BabyCenter are owned by Everyday Health, though they operate as separate apps with different branding, content teams, and features. It's an underreported detail that's worth knowing before you assume you're getting two independent takes: both run on the same ad-supported business model and both have disclosed selling personal data to third parties for targeted advertising.

Is Ovia free?

Not on its own. The standalone Ovia Cycle & Pregnancy Tracker runs roughly $150 per year or $22 per month, with no permanent free tier, though pricing and offers can change, so verify the current price in-app before deciding. Many users get it at no direct cost because Ovia (owned by Labcorp) is offered as an employer or health-plan benefit. If you don't have access through work or insurance, it's the most expensive of the three.

Which pregnancy app has the best privacy: Ovia, What to Expect, or BabyCenter?

None of the three is a clean privacy story, but the concerns are different in kind. What to Expect and BabyCenter, both owned by Everyday Health, run on an ad-supported model and have disclosed selling personal data to third parties for targeted advertising. BabyCenter has also been named in reporting on data broker practices. Ovia doesn't sell ads the same way, but employer-sponsored versions of the app can share de-identified health data with the employer or insurer providing it, which is its own tradeoff. Read the current privacy policy for whichever app you're considering, especially if you're using an employer-provided version.

Which is best for community support during pregnancy?

BabyCenter, generally. Its Birth Club groups organize members by due date month, so you're talking to people at the exact same stage as you, and it's completely free with no paywall. What to Expect has a Moms Forum that's also active and well-established. Ovia's community is smaller and more tracking-adjacent than either.

Can I use more than one of these apps at once?

A lot of people do. A common combination is BabyCenter or What to Expect for content and community, paired with a dedicated tracking app for kick counts, contraction timing, symptoms, and any lifestyle data you want to keep alongside your pregnancy. There's no rule against running two apps, and each does a different job well.

How does Go Go Gaia compare to Ovia, What to Expect, and BabyCenter?

Go Go Gaia is a tracking app, closer in spirit to Ovia than to the two content and community apps, but broader: it tracks pregnancy alongside 350+ other health metrics (nutrition, mood, sleep, fitness) in one app, with 1-click logging and a free tier. It doesn't have Ovia's food safety lookup, What to Expect's editorial library, or BabyCenter's Birth Clubs, so most people pair it with one of those rather than replacing them.
