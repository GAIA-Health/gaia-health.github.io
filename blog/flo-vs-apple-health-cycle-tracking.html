Flo vs Apple Health Cycle Tracking: What You Give Up (2026)

Your iPhone already tracks your period. Apple Health's Cycle Tracking is free, built in, and private by default, so the real question isn't whether it works. It's whether it does enough for you specifically. This guide compares what Apple Health logs and predicts against what a dedicated app like Flo adds, walks through when the native option is genuinely enough, and where a third option fits if you want more than either gives you.

Apple Health Cycle Tracking: What You Get

Cycle Tracking lives inside the built-in Health app on iPhone, with a matching standalone app on Apple Watch. There's nothing to download and nothing to set up beyond turning it on, which is a real advantage if you'd rather not add another app to your phone.

Features

  • Period logging with flow level (light, medium, heavy) and spotting
  • A fixed list of roughly a dozen or so loggable symptoms, including cramps, headaches, acne, fatigue, and mood changes
  • Cycle-length and next-period predictions based on your logged history
  • Retrospective ovulation estimates, using nightly wrist temperature on Apple Watch Series 8 or later, or any Apple Watch Ultra model
  • Support for logging ovulation test results and basal body temperature manually
  • Irregular, infrequent, or prolonged cycle notifications
  • Data stored on-device, end-to-end encrypted with iCloud backup enabled

Strengths

It's already there. No account to create, no app to download, no ads to click past. If you have an iPhone, you have this.

Privacy is the default, not an upgrade. Cycle Tracking data stays on your device and is end-to-end encrypted when iCloud backup is turned on, meaning Apple itself can't read it. There's no advertising model built around your cycle data here.

The ovulation estimate is a genuinely useful feature if you already wear an Apple Watch to sleep. Series 8 and later, plus all Ultra models, use nightly wrist temperature to detect the small biphasic shift that often follows ovulation, and combine that with your logged data to estimate which day ovulation likely occurred. Apple says it typically takes about two cycles of consistent nightly wear before an estimate is available.

Limitations

The symptom list is short and fixed. You can log from a set list of roughly a dozen or so symptoms. You can't add a custom symptom, and there's no free-text notes field for context.

No correlation insights. Apple Health can show you the data you logged, but it won't tell you that your headaches cluster in the days before your period, or that your sleep drops in the luteal phase. You have to notice patterns yourself.

The ovulation estimate is retrospective, not predictive. It tells you ovulation likely already happened, not that it's about to. And it depends on wearing an Apple Watch to sleep every night, which not everyone wants to do.

No community, no educational content, no partner sharing. It's a logging and prediction tool, not a place to read about your cycle or ask other people questions.

Flo: What You Get

Flo is the most-used period and pregnancy tracker in the category, with around 77 million active users. Where Apple Health is a quiet, built-in utility, Flo is a full app built around logging, learning, and community.

Features

  • Cycle tracking with AI-powered predictions
  • A much longer symptom list than Apple Health's, including custom tracking options
  • Thousands of health articles reviewed by medical professionals
  • Secret Chats for anonymous community discussions
  • AI chatbot for cycle and health questions (Flo Premium)
  • Pregnancy mode with week-by-week tracking, included free
  • Partner sharing for joint tracking
  • Apple Watch companion app

Strengths

Symptom logging goes well beyond Apple Health's fixed list. If you want to track something specific to you, Flo generally has more room for it.

The community is the largest in the category. With around 77 million active users, Flo's Secret Chats connect you to millions of other people who've likely dealt with whatever you're logging.

The content library is extensive. Thousands of medically reviewed articles cover cycle, fertility, and general health topics, something Apple Health doesn't attempt at all.

Limitations

Privacy is the tradeoff. Flo settled with the FTC in 2021 for sharing period and pregnancy data with Facebook and Google without user consent. A related class action against Flo, Google, and Flurry has preliminary court approval for a combined $59.5 million settlement, with a final approval hearing set for October 2026. Flo has since added Anonymous Mode and says it has changed its practices, but it's still a different starting posture than Apple Health's on-device default.

Deeper insights sit behind Flo Premium. The AI chatbot and some advanced features require a subscription, and free users see regular upgrade prompts.

No wrist-temperature ovulation estimate the way Apple Health has. Flo has an Apple Watch companion app, but the retrospective ovulation feature is specific to Apple's own Cycle Tracking.

Pricing

Free: Cycle and symptom tracking, pregnancy mode, community access. Includes ads.

Flo Premium: ~$59.99/year. Adds the AI assistant, an ad-free experience, and full content library access.

Download: Available on iOS and Android

When Apple Health Is Genuinely Enough

This part matters more than most comparison guides let on: for a real slice of people, Apple Health's Cycle Tracking is not a lesser option you're settling for. It's the right tool for the job.

If your main goal is knowing roughly when your next period is coming, and your cycle is fairly regular, logging a handful of periods into Apple Health will get you a reasonable prediction without installing anything.

If privacy is your top priority and you'd rather your cycle data never touch a third-party server at all, Apple Health's on-device, end-to-end-encrypted default is hard to beat. There's no company to trust here beyond Apple itself.

If you already wear an Apple Watch to sleep, the retrospective ovulation estimate is a real feature you'd otherwise have to track manually with a basal body thermometer. You're getting something dedicated apps without wrist-temperature access can't replicate on their own.

If you don't want community features, articles, or an AI assistant, and would honestly rather your period tracker not try to be anything more than a logging tool, the simplicity is a feature, not a gap.

Where Apple Health tends to fall short is when you want to understand your cycle in the context of everything else going on with your body: sleep, mood, workouts, nutrition. It logs what you tell it and predicts your cycle from that, but it doesn't connect the dots across categories. That's the gap a dedicated app, whether that's Flo's larger feature set or an all-in-one tracker, is built to fill.

Privacy: What You Give Up Either Way

Privacy is usually the deciding factor when people compare a built-in tool against a dedicated app, so it's worth being specific about what each one actually does.

Apple Health's Cycle Tracking data is stored on-device and end-to-end encrypted when iCloud backup is turned on, which means Apple itself can't read it. There's no advertising business built around this data, and nothing is shared with third parties by default. This is the strongest privacy posture of the three apps in this comparison, largely because it isn't trying to be anything other than a private logging tool.

Flo's privacy record is more complicated. Flo settled with the FTC in 2021 for sharing period and pregnancy data with Facebook and Google without user consent. A related class action against Flo, Google, and Flurry has preliminary court approval for a combined $59.5 million settlement, with a final approval hearing set for October 2026. Flo has since added Anonymous Mode, which lets you use the app without attaching your name or email, and says it has changed its data-sharing practices. Whether that's enough for you is a personal call, but it's worth knowing before you log sensitive cycle or pregnancy data into any app, including ours.

Neither option is a reason to avoid tracking your cycle altogether. It's a reason to pick the option whose privacy posture actually matches what you're comfortable with, and to read an app's current privacy policy before you rely on anything written about it, including this article.

Which Should You Choose?

There's no single "best" answer here. It depends on what you actually want out of tracking your cycle.

Stick with Apple Health if:

  • You want free, private period predictions with nothing to download
  • On-device, encrypted storage matters more to you than extra features
  • You already wear an Apple Watch to sleep and want the ovulation estimate
  • You don't want community features, articles, or an AI assistant
  • A short, fixed symptom list covers what you actually want to log

Choose Flo if:

  • You want access to the largest community of people tracking their cycles
  • A large, medically reviewed content library matters to you
  • You want a longer, more customizable symptom list than Apple Health offers
  • You need Android support
  • You're comfortable with Flo's privacy history (or willing to use Anonymous Mode)

Choose Go Go Gaia if:

  • You want your cycle tracked alongside mood, sleep, and nutrition, not on its own
  • Correlation insights (how your cycle relates to other symptoms) matter to you
  • You track with a wearable and want that data connected automatically
  • You're on iOS (or okay using the web app on other platforms)
  • Community and editorial content aren't what you're looking for from a tracking app

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a period tracking app, or is Apple Health enough?

It depends on what you want from tracking. Apple Health's Cycle Tracking is free, private by default, and covers the basics well: period logging, a limited symptom list, and cycle-length predictions, plus retrospective ovulation estimates if you wear an Apple Watch to sleep. If that's all you need, it's genuinely enough and you don't need a separate app. A dedicated app like Flo adds a larger symptom list, correlation insights, community features, and content. If you want to see patterns across symptoms, moods, and other health metrics, or you want community and educational content, a dedicated app adds real value on top of what's built in.

How accurate are Apple Health's ovulation estimates?

Apple Watch (Series 8 or later, or any Ultra model) uses your nightly wrist temperature to detect a biphasic temperature shift, the small rise that often follows ovulation, and combines it with your logged cycle data to estimate which day ovulation likely occurred. It's a retrospective estimate, meaning you find out after the fact, not a real-time prediction. Apple says the feature typically needs about two cycles of consistent nightly wear before it can generate an estimate, and it notes that ovulation can occur without a detectable temperature shift. Treat it as a helpful data point, not a guarantee.

What symptoms can you log in Apple Health's Cycle Tracking?

Apple Health's Cycle Tracking supports a limited, fixed list of roughly a dozen or so symptoms, things like cramps, headaches, acne, fatigue, and mood changes, along with flow level, spotting, and results from ovulation tests or basal body temperature readings. You can't add custom symptoms or track things outside that list, and it won't show you how a logged symptom correlates with anything else in the Health app. Dedicated period apps generally support a longer, more specific symptom list and some, including Flo and Go Go Gaia, will show you patterns between symptoms and other tracked data over time.

Is Flo or Apple Health better for privacy?

Apple Health has the stronger privacy starting point. Cycle Tracking data is stored on-device and end-to-end encrypted when iCloud backup is on, meaning Apple itself can't read it, and it isn't shared with advertisers by default. Flo settled with the FTC in 2021 for sharing period and pregnancy data with Facebook and Google without user consent. A related class action against Flo, Google, and Flurry has preliminary court approval for a combined $59.5 million settlement, with a final approval hearing set for October 2026. Flo has since added Anonymous Mode and says it has changed its practices, but the track record is still worth knowing if privacy is your main concern.

Can Apple Health Cycle Tracking sync with a period tracker app?

Many period tracker apps, including Flo and Go Go Gaia, can read from and write to Apple Health, so your cycle data doesn't have to live in only one place. This means you can use Apple's built-in wrist temperature and ovulation estimate features while still logging your day-to-day symptoms in a dedicated app with a longer symptom list and correlation features. Check an app's Health integration settings before assuming data flows both directions automatically.

Does Apple Health Cycle Tracking work without an Apple Watch?

It does. You can log periods, flow, and symptoms directly in the Health app on iPhone, and you'll still get cycle-length and period predictions based on that logged data. What you lose without an Apple Watch (Series 8 or later, or an Ultra model worn to sleep) is the retrospective ovulation estimate, since that feature depends on nightly wrist temperature readings. The core period-logging and prediction features don't require a watch at all.
