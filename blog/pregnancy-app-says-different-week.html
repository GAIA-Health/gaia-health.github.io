Why Your Pregnancy App Says a Different Week Than Your Doctor

You walk out of an appointment a week or more "off" from what your app has been telling you all along. This guide covers why that happens: how LMP counting differs from ultrasound redating, why apps default to your last period, and why some won't let you edit your week at all.

By Go Go Gaia Team • Published August 5, 2026 • 7 min read • Pregnancy

Educational content about how pregnancy apps calculate dates, not medical advice. Your provider's dating always governs your care.

Quick Answer: Why the Mismatch Happens

Most pregnancy apps calculate your week from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP). That's a standard, widely used starting point. But clinics frequently adjust, or "redate," that estimate after a first-trimester ultrasound, which can measure gestational age more precisely than a calendar count.

When your app and your chart disagree, it's almost always this: the app is still counting from LMP, and your doctor's number reflects an ultrasound redate. Your provider's dating is what governs your care, not the app's calendar math.

It's a jarring moment. You've been watching your app count up week by week, maybe even reading the weekly size comparisons, and then your doctor tells you that you're actually a few days or over a week ahead or behind that number. Did the app get it wrong? Did your doctor make a mistake? Usually, neither. They're just using two different, both-valid, ways of counting.

Here's what's actually going on, and what it means for the number you should trust.

How Apps Calculate Your Week: LMP Counting

When you first set up a pregnancy app, it typically asks for the first day of your last period. From there, it counts forward assuming a standard cycle, usually 28 days with ovulation around day 14. Pregnancy is measured in "gestational age," which by convention starts counting from that last period date, not from conception.

This method is simple, doesn't require any test results, and works reasonably well when your cycle is close to that 28-day assumption and you know your last period date accurately. It's also exactly how most apps generate their week-by-week countdown, due date estimate, and trimester markers.

The catch: not everyone has a 28-day cycle, not everyone ovulates on day 14, and not everyone remembers their last period date with certainty. Any of those can throw the LMP estimate off by several days without anyone doing anything wrong.

How Clinics Redate: Ultrasound Measurement

Once you have a first-trimester ultrasound, your provider can measure the size of the embryo or fetus and compare it against typical growth curves for that stage. In early pregnancy, this measurement tends to be a more precise way to estimate gestational age than a calendar count from your last period, especially if your cycle isn't a textbook 28 days.

When the ultrasound estimate and the LMP estimate differ by enough, many clinics will "redate" the pregnancy, meaning they update your due date and week count on your chart to match the ultrasound. From that point on, your provider's records reflect the ultrasound-based date, not the original LMP calculation.

This is a normal, well-established part of prenatal care. It isn't a sign that anything went wrong with your original estimate. It's simply a more precise measurement becoming available partway through.

Why Your App Doesn't Automatically Know About the Redate

Here's the mechanical reason for the mismatch: your app has no way of knowing your ultrasound results happened unless you tell it. It's still running on the last period date you entered at setup, quietly counting forward week by week, with no idea your provider adjusted anything.

Some apps make this easy to fix. Look in the app's due date or pregnancy settings for an option to manually update your due date, and entering the date your doctor gave you should bring the app's week count back in line with your chart.

Other apps calculate the week directly from the LMP date you entered and don't offer a way to override it. That's usually because the app uses that same start date to drive other features, like weekly content or trimester markers, and letting you edit just the week number without touching the underlying date could create inconsistencies elsewhere in the app. If your app doesn't have an obvious edit option, that's often why, not a bug.

Which Date Should You Actually Go By?

Your doctor's dating governs your prenatal care: testing windows, appointment scheduling, and the due date on your medical chart all follow whatever your provider has on file, especially after an ultrasound redate. If your app and your chart disagree, the chart is the one to plan around.

That said, your app is still useful for the day-to-day of pregnancy tracking, logging symptoms, weight, appointments, and how you're feeling week to week. It just may need a manual nudge to reflect the date your provider actually uses. The Bottom Line

A pregnancy app and a doctor's chart can disagree by days or over a week, and it's almost always because the app is counting from your last period while your provider has redated based on an ultrasound. Neither number was wrong for what it was measuring. Your provider's dating is the one that governs your care. Update your app's due date if it lets you, and lean on your doctor's number for anything that matters, appointments, testing windows, and your actual due date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information about app mechanics. Not medical advice. Your provider's dating governs your care.

Why does my pregnancy app say a different week than my doctor?

Most pregnancy apps count your week from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP), a standard dating method. Your doctor may use that same starting point early on, but often adjusts it after an ultrasound, since ultrasound measurements in the first trimester are considered a more precise way to estimate gestational age. When the two dates disagree, it's usually because the app is still going by LMP while your chart has been redated by ultrasound.

Which date is right, my app's or my doctor's?

Your doctor's dating is what governs your prenatal care, testing windows, and due date on your medical chart. If an ultrasound redated your pregnancy, that's the number to go by, not what an app calculated from your last period. Apps are useful for day-to-day tracking, but they don't have access to your ultrasound measurements unless you manually update them.

Why won't my pregnancy app let me change my week?

Some apps calculate your week automatically from the last period date you entered and don't offer a way to override it, since editing it manually can throw off other calculations the app makes from that same start date, like trimester markers or weekly content. Other apps do allow you to manually update your due date or week after an ultrasound. If yours doesn't, check the app's due date or pregnancy settings, since the option is sometimes tucked away rather than missing entirely.

How much can LMP dating and ultrasound dating differ?

It varies. If your cycles are a consistent 28 days and you remember your last period date accurately, LMP dating and ultrasound dating are often close. If your cycles are longer, shorter, or irregular, or ovulation happened later than a 28-day cycle would assume, the two methods can differ by several days to more than a week. That gap is exactly why clinics often use the ultrasound date as the more reliable one once it's available.

Should I update my due date in my app myself?

If your app has a setting to manually enter or update your due date, and your doctor has given you an ultrasound-based date, updating it there can keep your app's week count matching your chart. If you're not sure which date to enter, your doctor's office can confirm the exact due date they have on file.
