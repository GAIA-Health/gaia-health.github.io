Natural Cycles vs Oura Ring: Cycle Tracking Compared

Natural Cycles and Oura Ring get compared a lot, but they're not really competing for the same job, and they aren't strictly separate either. Since 2022, a compatible Oura Ring can feed its overnight temperature readings straight into Natural Cycles, inside the wearable-temperature capability Natural Cycles is FDA-cleared for.[3] This guide covers what each one measures and costs on its own, what changes when you connect them, and where a general tracking app still has a role even if you use both.

Full Transparency

This comparison is published by Holland Neurotech Inc., the company behind Go Go Gaia. We've mentioned Go Go Gaia where it's relevant to what a general logging app adds. We've done our best to describe Natural Cycles and Oura fairly based on their own published materials. This article does not offer guidance on whether to use either product as birth control. That decision, and any question about contraception, belongs with you and your healthcare provider.

Quick Answer

Oura Ring is a health-tracking ring that measures temperature, sleep, heart rate, and HRV, with its own cycle insights built on top. Natural Cycles is a fertility app that turns a confirmed temperature shift into a daily fertility status, and it's the one of the two with FDA clearance as a birth control method.[1] They aren't fighting for the same use case. In fact, Oura is one of the devices Natural Cycles can pull temperature data from directly.[3]

  • If you want broad health and sleep tracking with a temperature-based cycle estimate included: Oura Ring covers that on its own.
  • If you're asking which of the two carries FDA clearance as a birth control method: that's Natural Cycles, with or without a ring. This guide doesn't cover whether or how to use it that way.
  • If you want symptoms, mood, habits, and lab results tracked alongside whatever your ring or app measures: A general tracker like Go Go Gaia fills that gap.

Why This Isn't Really a Rivalry

Search "Natural Cycles vs Oura" and you'll find people treating them like competing period trackers. They aren't built for the same thing, and they aren't mutually exclusive.

Oura Ring is a general health wearable. It measures your temperature, sleep stages, resting heart rate, and HRV overnight, and packages some of that into its own Cycle Insights and Readiness Score, covered in more depth in our breakdown of what Oura's cycle features actually measure. Oura also has its own Fertile Window feature, described by the company as an aid for trying to conceive, not a tool to prevent pregnancy.[4]

Natural Cycles is a fertility app, not a wearable. It was the first app cleared by the FDA as a method of birth control, in 2018, based on clinical data showing 93% effectiveness with typical use and 98% with perfect use.[1] It started with a manual oral thermometer and still supports one, but it now also accepts temperature data from a handful of wearables, Oura Ring among them, plus Apple Watch and Garmin, following a separate 2023 FDA clearance for the Apple Watch integration.[5]

So the real question isn't which app to pick. It's what each one does alone, and what changes if you connect them. That's what the rest of this guide covers. If you're weighing Natural Cycles against a period tracker instead, our Flo vs Natural Cycles and Clue vs Natural Cycles comparisons cover that decision.

Oura Ring on Its Own

Oura is a smart ring, not an app you download for free. The ring itself is the product, and the software is what you get once you're wearing it.

What It Measures and Shows

  • Overnight finger temperature, sampled roughly every minute and reported as a deviation from your personal baseline[3]
  • Sleep stages, sleep duration, and a nightly Sleep score
  • Resting heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV)
  • A daily Readiness score built from those overnight signals
  • Cycle Insights: a cycle-phase estimate and symptom-adjacent context, built from the same temperature and HRV data
  • A Fertile Window feature aimed at people trying to conceive, not at preventing pregnancy[4]

Strengths

It's a genuine health wearable, not just a cycle gadget. Sleep, HRV, and daily readiness are useful on their own, whether or not you're tracking a cycle at all. And the ring form factor has a real edge for temperature: a finger sensor sits closer to a stable reading than a wrist sensor and is less exposed to open air, which is part of why Natural Cycles chose to support it as a data source.[3]

Limitations

It isn't birth control. Oura's own materials are explicit that its Fertile Window feature is designed to help you try to conceive, not to prevent pregnancy.[4] Wearing the ring alone doesn't give you an FDA-cleared contraceptive method. The ring and the membership are also two separate costs, with deeper insights like Cycle Insights sitting behind the ongoing membership. And Oura reads what your body does passively overnight. There's no place to log cramps, mood, or what you ate.

Pricing

Ring: The Oura Ring 5 starts at $399 in Silver or Black, with premium finishes like Gold at $499, and the earlier Oura Ring 4 is still sold from $349, as of September 2026.[2]

Membership: $5.99/month or $69.99/year, with a free first month for new members, as of September 2026. Most of Oura's detailed insights, including Cycle Insights, require an active membership.[2]

Buy: Available at ouraring.com

Natural Cycles on Its Own

Natural Cycles is a subscription app built around one job: turning your temperature into a daily fertility status you can act on. It's not trying to be a general wellness tracker.

What It Measures and Shows

  • A daily green-day or red-day fertility status, based on basal body temperature
  • Confirmed ovulation detection, once a sustained temperature rise is identified
  • Modes for preventing pregnancy, planning pregnancy, following a pregnancy, postpartum, and perimenopause, all under one subscription
  • Compatibility with a manual oral thermometer, its own NC° Band, or a connected Oura Ring, Apple Watch, or Garmin device for automatic temperature sync[6]

Strengths

It measures rather than estimates. Because it reads temperature and confirms ovulation rather than projecting from a calendar, Natural Cycles holds FDA clearance to be used as birth control, with 93% effectiveness reported for typical use and 98% for perfect use.[1] This guide doesn't cover whether or how to use it that way. It's also flexible about how you take your temperature: a basic oral thermometer, the company's own wrist band, or a ring or watch you already own can all feed the same algorithm.[6]

Limitations

There's no ongoing free tier, just a trial period. And it's narrow by design: outside of its fertility and pregnancy modes, Natural Cycles doesn't track mood, general symptoms, nutrition, or fitness the way a broader health app does.

Pricing

Subscription: Annual or monthly plans, varying by promotion, as of September 2026. Natural Cycles doesn't post a single fixed USD price publicly, so this figure is approximate.[7] Expect the annual plan to land somewhere near $100, and the annual plan can include a free NC° Band.

Sign up: Available at naturalcycles.com

Using Them Together

This is the part most comparisons skip. If you already own an Oura Ring, you don't have to choose between it and Natural Cycles.

In 2022, Natural Cycles added a compatible Oura Ring (Gen 2 and later) as a temperature source for its fertility algorithm, inside the wearable-temperature capability the FDA had already cleared.[3] Once connected, your Oura Ring measures overnight finger temperature and syncs that trend to the Natural Cycles app each morning, so you skip the manual oral thermometer step entirely.[6] The algorithm also factors in overnight heart rate from the ring, which helps it account for nights when temperature ran high for a reason unrelated to your cycle, like a late night out.[6]

A few things are worth knowing about the combination. It's a one-way sync, meaning data flows from Oura into Natural Cycles but not back.[6] You still pay for both, since an active Oura Membership and a separate Natural Cycles subscription are both required.[6] You keep Oura's other features, since Sleep, Readiness, and Cycle Insights keep working alongside Natural Cycles. And the clearance sits with Natural Cycles, not the ring: Oura's own Fertile Window feature still isn't a birth control tool on its own.[3][4]

Whether the setup is worth it mostly comes down to whether you already own a ring. Buying one purely to feed Natural Cycles is a meaningfully bigger cost than a basic oral thermometer, for the convenience of skipping a manual reading every morning.

What Neither One Tracks

Whether you use Oura, Natural Cycles, or both together, you end up with a strong picture of temperature, sleep, and fertility status. What you don't get from either one is the rest of your day.

Neither product logs how you're actually feeling: mood, energy, cramps, headaches, or digestive symptoms. Neither tracks habits like hydration or workouts as part of a cycle-aware view, and neither pulls in lab results, so a blood panel or a genetic report sits in a separate PDF, disconnected from the temperature trend you're already tracking. That's a reasonable scope limit, not a flaw. Both products are built around a specific measurement, not a whole-health picture.

This is where a general logging app still has a role, even for someone fully set up with a ring and a fertility app. A tracker like Go Go Gaia can log symptoms, mood, and habits by hand, pull in wearable data from Apple Watch, Oura, or Garmin for context, and hold lab results alongside everything else, so a dip in temperature or HRV sits next to the fact that you also slept badly and had a stressful week. It doesn't replace what Oura or Natural Cycles does. It fills in the parts neither one was built to cover. Frequently Asked Questions

Can Oura Ring be used with Natural Cycles?

Yes. Natural Cycles added support for temperature data from a compatible Oura Ring (Gen 2 and later) in 2022, feeding it into its FDA-cleared algorithm. Your ring syncs its overnight temperature trend to the Natural Cycles app each morning, so you skip a separate oral thermometer. It's one-way: data logged in Natural Cycles doesn't flow back into the Oura app.

Do I need both an Oura and a Natural Cycles subscription?

Yes. You need an Oura Ring plus an active Oura Membership, and a separate Natural Cycles subscription. Neither discounts the other's price for using the integration, though Natural Cycles has offered sign-up discounts for Oura members at times, so check current promotions on naturalcycles.com.

Is Oura's own cycle tracking the same as Natural Cycles?

No. Oura's Fertile Window feature is built from the same temperature and heart rate signals, but the company is explicit it's designed as an aid for trying to conceive, not for preventing pregnancy. Natural Cycles is the separate app that holds FDA clearance for that use, so wearing an Oura Ring alone doesn't give you an FDA-cleared birth control method.

What does Natural Cycles do that Oura can't?

Natural Cycles turns a confirmed temperature shift into a daily green-day or red-day fertility status and holds FDA clearance as birth control, with 93% effectiveness for typical use and 98% for perfect use. Oura reports temperature trends, sleep, HRV, and readiness, and has its own cycle and fertile-window insights, but no contraceptive clearance of its own.

Do I still need a period tracking app if I use Natural Cycles or Oura?

It depends what you want tracked. Both center on temperature and a small set of biometric signals. Neither logs mood, symptoms, nutrition, habits, or lab results, so neither gives you a whole-health view alongside your cycle. A general tracking app like Go Go Gaia fills that gap, though it isn't a substitute for either product's specific job.
