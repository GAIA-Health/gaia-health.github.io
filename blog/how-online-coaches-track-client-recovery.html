How Online Coaches Track Client Recovery

A look at how online coaches read sleep, readiness, training load, and cycle phase together to get an honest picture of a client's recovery, instead of reacting to one wearable score at a time.

By Go Go Gaia Team • Published August 26, 2026 • 8 min read • For Coaches

Educational content for online coaches and trainers, not medical advice. This guide covers recovery and training decisions. It isn't a guide to diagnosing or treating any condition, which stays with a client's physician.

Quick Answer

Tracking a client's recovery means reading her sleep, a readiness or HRV-style score, her training load, and for female clients, her cycle phase, together across the week rather than reacting to any single number. Wearables hand you the raw signals. What most coaches are missing is one place to see those signals lined up against the training you actually assigned, without asking a client to screenshot four different apps into a group chat.

A client's readiness score drops from 78 to 51 overnight. Do you back off her session, push through as planned, or ask her what happened first? If all you have is that one number, you're guessing. If you also know she slept five hours after a late flight, or that she's three days out from her period, the number stops being a mystery and starts being useful.

This guide covers what a wearable readiness or HRV score actually tells a coach and where the evidence runs out, the screenshot-relay problem that keeps most coaches from seeing this data at all, why cycle phase is the recovery overlay most tools leave out, a lean weekly review flow that doesn't eat your whole Sunday, and how a consent-based portal replaces the relay entirely.

What "Recovery" Means When You're Coaching Someone Else's Body

Recovery isn't one thing you can read off a dashboard. It's the combination of how well a client slept, how much stress her nervous system is carrying, how hard her recent training has been relative to what she's built up to, and for clients who cycle, where she is in that cycle. None of those alone tells you much. Together, they start to look like a picture you can coach from.

The reason this matters for online coaching specifically is that you don't see your client train. You're not in the room to notice she looked flat on set three or that she seemed off during warmup. Whatever you know about her recovery has to come from data, from what she tells you, or from both. Getting that data flow right is most of the job.

What Wearables Actually Give a Coach

Devices like Whoop, Oura, Garmin, and Apple Watch estimate recovery from a handful of proxy signals: resting heart rate, heart rate variability (HRV), and sleep duration and stages. Each brand runs those inputs through its own formula to produce a single readiness or recovery score, and the exact math is usually proprietary.

HRV is the input coaches hear about most, and for good reason. It's widely used as a marker of training stress, though what a single day's number means for one person is much less settled. An HRV number on a given morning moves with sleep timing, alcohol, hydration, travel, illness, and plain day-to-day variability, not just training load, so a single dip usually means less than coaches want it to mean.

That's the honest limit of the evidence: HRV and readiness scores are more reliable as a rolling trend across a week or two than as a single-day verdict. If you already program around how a client's heart rate response shifts across her training week, this is the same logic applied to recovery. One reading is a data point. A pattern is a signal.

The Screenshot-Relay Problem

Here's the gap most coaching setups run into: the wearable data lives on the client's wrist and in her app. Unless she opens her dashboard, screenshots it, and sends it over, you never see it. In practice that only happens when a number looks notably good or bad, so you're working from a biased sample. The forgettable, in-between days, which are most days, never reach you at all.

Even when a client does forward something, it arrives without context. A screenshot of a 61 readiness score doesn't tell you what her sleep looked like the night before, whether her training load has been climbing for two weeks, or whether she's in the days leading up to her period. You're left asking follow-up questions during a check-in call about a number from four days ago that she may not remember well enough to answer.

Cycle Phase, the Recovery Overlay Most Tools Skip

Most consumer wearables weren't built with a female client's cycle in mind. A readiness or HRV score that dips in the days before a period gets read by the device the same way a dip from poor sleep or heavy training would: as worse recovery, full stop. The device has no way to tell a coach that this pattern shows up for plenty of clients in the late luteal phase and doesn't necessarily mean anything went wrong.

That's a real blind spot if you coach female clients and only have the wearable score to go on. Knowing where a client is in her cycle turns a confusing dip into a much more ordinary one, or tells you it's worth a closer look because it doesn't line up with phase at all. A Lean Weekly Recovery Review

You don't need to check every client's numbers every day. A short weekly pass across three things covers most of it: how her sleep trended, how her training load compares to what you assigned, and, for clients who track it, whether cycle phase explains anything unusual in the other two. Anything she raised herself, a rough week, an injury tweak, poor sleep from travel, goes in the same review instead of getting reconstructed from memory later.

This pairs well with a structured weekly check-in instead of a running group-chat thread. Frequently Asked Questions

What do wearables actually measure when they show a recovery or readiness score?

A wearable readiness score is built from a handful of proxy signals, most often resting heart rate, heart rate variability (HRV), and sleep duration and quality, run through the device's own scoring formula. It's an estimate of how a client's nervous system responded to the last day or two, not a direct measurement of how recovered she actually is. Different brands weight these inputs differently, so a 72 on one device and a 72 on another aren't reading the same thing.

How much should one low readiness day change a training plan?

A single low reading is weak evidence on its own. Day-to-day HRV is noisy for any one person, shifting with things like sleep timing, alcohol, travel, illness, or stress that have nothing to do with training. The signal worth acting on is a client's readiness or sleep trending down across several days in a row, not one score that dipped after a bad night.

Does the coach portal show a client's raw HRV number?

The portal doesn't surface a raw HRV figure. What you see on a client's shared profile is her sleep, a readiness score, her workouts and training load, her cycle phase, and BBT trend under her vitals, all on one timeline once she's invited you in. That's enough to spot a pattern worth asking about without needing the underlying wearable math yourself.

Does the portal flag missed sessions or compliance automatically?

It doesn't, and that's worth saying plainly. There's no automatic compliance score or missed-session alert built into the roster right now. What you get is the readiness and recovery picture described above, plus flags you raise yourself when something in a client's data catches your attention. A missed session still needs a conversation, not a notification.

Does cycle phase actually change how a low readiness score should be read?

For clients who track their cycle, phase gives you context a wearable alone doesn't have. A dip in readiness or HRV in the days before a period is a pattern plenty of coaches and clients recognize, and seeing that a low score lines up with the late luteal phase is a different conversation than a low score with no clear cause. It's context, not a diagnosis, and it doesn't replace watching how she actually feels and performs.
