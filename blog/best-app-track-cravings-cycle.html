What's Known About Cravings and Your Cycle

Appetite and food cravings shifting across the menstrual cycle is a well-documented pattern. Cleveland Clinic lists food cravings and changes in appetite among the recognized symptoms of premenstrual syndrome, alongside things like bloating and mood changes[1].

The leading explanation involves hormones and brain chemistry working together. In the days before a period, estrogen and progesterone drop, and that shift appears to affect serotonin, a neurotransmitter tied to mood and appetite regulation. Lower serotonin activity is thought to be part of why cravings for sweet or high-carb foods tend to cluster in the late luteal phase, the stretch of days right before a period starts.

That said, this is still an active area of research. Study sizes are often small, methods vary, and how strongly someone feels this pattern differs a lot from person to person. Some people notice almost nothing. Others notice it every single month, like clockwork. That range is exactly why tracking your own cycles tells you more than any general statistic can.

One thing worth naming clearly: a craving is not a decision, and noticing one isn't a problem to fix. It's just information, the same way noticing you're tired or noticing a headache is information. This guide treats it that way throughout, and if you want a deeper look at cycle-related mood shifts that sometimes travel alongside cravings, our PMS vs PMDD guide covers that territory.

The 5 Apps Compared

The five apps below split into three groups: nutrition-first loggers built for diet and macro goals (MyFitnessPal, Cronometer), a fully customizable correlation tool (Bearable), and cycle-first trackers that can hold a cravings tag (Clue, Go Go Gaia). None was built for cravings-and-cycle tracking specifically. The right pick depends on whether detailed nutrition data, flexible correlation, or cycle context matters most to you.

For Detailed Food Logging: MyFitnessPal

Best if you want: The biggest food-logging database around, with a barcode scanner and a massive library of pre-entered foods and restaurant meals.

Key Features

  • Huge food database with barcode scanning for packaged foods
  • Macro and calorie tracking against a daily target
  • Recipe importer and meal logging by photo
  • Community features, including groups and challenges
  • Integrates with several fitness trackers and apps

Strengths

  • The food database is unmatched. If you eat it, MyFitnessPal probably has it logged already
  • Fast entry once you've searched for a food a few times, since it remembers your history
  • Large, active community if that kind of accountability helps you
  • Cross-platform (iOS and Android)

Limitations

  • MyFitnessPal is built for nutrition and diet goals, tracking calories and macros against a target. That's a different job than spotting a cravings pattern against your cycle, and it has no cycle tracking of its own
  • No way to tag a craving itself, only what you actually ate
  • Free users see regular prompts to upgrade to Premium

Who Should Choose This

  • You're already logging food for nutrition reasons and want a cycle app running alongside it separately
  • Detailed calorie and macro data matters more to you than cycle context
  • You want the largest possible food database

Pricing

Free (with ads and upgrade prompts), Premium ~$19.99/month or ~$79.99/year. Available on iOS and Android.

When Another App Is the Better Choice

Go Go Gaia isn't the right pick for everyone reading this, and several of these apps win outright for specific situations. Here's where we'd honestly point you elsewhere, in two or three sentences each.

Choose MyFitnessPal if detailed food logging is the actual goal

If you're already tracking nutrition for its own reasons, whether that's macros, calories, or just a habit you've built over years, MyFitnessPal's food database is bigger than anything on this list. You'll run a separate cycle tracker alongside it, but for pure food logging it's hard to beat.

Choose Cronometer if micronutrients are what you're watching

If iron, magnesium, or another specific nutrient is the thing you actually care about, Cronometer's verified nutrition data goes deeper than any other app here. It won't tell you anything about your cycle, so you'd still need a second app for that side of things.

Choose Bearable if cravings are one factor among many

If you're already tracking sleep, stress, migraines, or other symptoms and want cravings folded into that same correlation view, Bearable's "Impacts" tool is the strongest analysis engine on this list. It's also on Android, which Go Go Gaia isn't. Budget an evening for setup and it pays off.

Choose Clue if you want the lightest cycle-first option

If you mostly want a clean period tracker with a built-in cravings tag and nothing more elaborate, Clue does that well, and its German base under GDPR is a real point in its favor if data handling is a priority for you. You'll review patterns yourself rather than getting computed insights.

What to Log to See Your Own Pattern

Log four things daily: a cravings tag, a rough appetite rating, sleep, and your period dates. Keep each one to a tap or a simple rating, and keep it up for 2 cycles, ideally 3. That's the whole method. Here's how to make it stick:

  1. Make logging stupidly easy. One tap for "craving today" is enough. If your daily log takes more than 30 seconds, you'll quit by week three. Design for your busiest day, not your calmest one.
  2. Define your own terms once, up front. Decide what counts as a craving worth logging for you (a fleeting thought? something you acted on? a specific food?) and keep the meaning consistent. Stable labels are what make a pattern readable later.
  3. Track sleep too, even roughly. A rough night can shift appetite on its own, separate from where you are in your cycle. Apps that pull sleep from a wearable automatically remove this step entirely.
  4. Anchor everything to cycle day. The pattern you're hunting is "the same cycle week, repeating." After 2 or 3 cycles, compare your late luteal week, the week before each period, across months and look for repeats.
  5. Note the outliers. Travel, a stressful deadline, a holiday. A one-line note saves you from misreading a chaotic week as a hormonal one.
  6. Bring the record to your doctor or dietitian if it's useful. "Cravings show up the same week every month, and here are three cycles of data" is a more productive starting point than trying to describe it from memory. What you do with that pattern is a conversation for their office, not for an app.

A tracking app does two of these steps for you: a 1-tap cravings tag like Clue's or Go Go Gaia's keeps the friction near zero, and correlation insights handle the cycle-day comparison automatically, surfacing things like whether your cravings tags cluster in the days right before your period. If detailed nutrition matters more to you than pattern-spotting, MyFitnessPal or Cronometer are built for that job instead, and you'd pair either one with a separate cycle tracker.

Frequently Asked Questions

The short version: appetite and food cravings shifting before a period is a well-documented pattern, no dedicated cravings-plus-cycle app exists yet, and 2 to 3 tracked cycles is what it takes to see your own version of it. The details:

Why do I crave sweets or carbs before my period?

The leading explanation ties this to falling estrogen and progesterone in the days before a period, which appears to affect serotonin and other appetite-related signals in the brain. Cleveland Clinic lists food cravings and appetite changes among the recognized symptoms of PMS. The pattern is well documented at a population level, but the timing and intensity are personal, which is why tracking your own cycles is the clearest way to see your version of it.

Is craving chocolate before my period normal?

Craving sweet or high-carb foods in the days before a period is a commonly reported pattern, and Cleveland Clinic lists food cravings and appetite changes among the recognized symptoms of PMS. What counts as normal varies a lot between individuals, so the more useful question is whether the pattern is consistent for you, which a couple of cycles of tracking can answer.

Should I track what I eat or just my cravings?

Either works, and the right choice depends on what you're trying to see. A simple craving or appetite tag next to your cycle day is enough to spot timing patterns. Logging actual meals in more detail, the way MyFitnessPal or Cronometer does, is more useful if you're already tracking nutrition for another reason and want to layer cycle context on top.

How many cycles do I need to track before a pattern shows up?

Plan on at least 2 full cycles, and 3 if you can manage it. A single cycle can be thrown off by travel, illness, or a stressful week that has nothing to do with hormones. Comparing the same cycle week across 2 or 3 months is what turns a one-off observation into a real pattern.

Can a food app help me stop craving certain foods?

Tracking apps are built to record patterns, not to change what you crave or steer your eating in any direction. What they can do is show you when cravings tend to show up in your cycle, which is useful information on its own, separate from any decision about what to do with it.

Is there an app for tracking cravings and my period at the same time?

Several apps can do this, though none was built for this exact pairing from the ground up. Clue supports custom cravings tags inside a cycle-first tracker, Bearable lets you build a custom cravings factor and correlate it with anything else you log, and Go Go Gaia tracks cycle, meals, cravings tags, mood, and sleep together in one app.

Can an app diagnose an eating disorder or hormonal imbalance?

Tracking apps record what you log, they don't diagnose anything. If your relationship with food feels stressful, difficult, or like it's taking up more space in your head than you want, that's worth bringing to a doctor, dietitian, or therapist who can actually help, regardless of what any app shows.

Final Thoughts

If your cravings show up on a schedule, you're not imagining it, and the research backs that up in broad strokes. The exact pattern is personal enough that no article can tell you yours. Two to three tracked cycles can.

Pick the app that matches what you're actually trying to see: MyFitnessPal for the biggest food database, Cronometer for micronutrient detail, Bearable for deep correlation across many factors, Clue for a light, privacy-first cycle tracker with a built-in cravings tag, and Go Go Gaia if you want cycle, meals, cravings, mood, and sleep together in one app.
