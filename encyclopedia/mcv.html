MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

MCV (mean corpuscular volume) is the average size of your red blood cells, reported as part of a complete blood count.[1] It's one of three red cell indices, alongside MCH (average hemoglobin per cell) and MCHC (average hemoglobin concentration per cell), that describe red cells beyond a simple count.[2]

What MCV Measures

MCV measures the average size and volume of the red blood cells in a sample.[1] MCH, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, is the average amount of hemoglobin inside a single red cell, and MCHC, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, is how concentrated that hemoglobin is relative to the cell's size.[2] Together the three are called the red cell indices. A red blood cell count tells you how many cells you have. The indices describe what those cells are like.

All three are averages across millions of cells, so they describe the sample as a whole, not any single cell. Hematology research uses size vocabulary built on MCV, calling cells smaller than typical microcytic and cells larger than typical macrocytic.[1] That grouping is descriptive. What any one person's numbers mean depends on their full labs and history, which is a conversation for their doctor. Iron connects to this picture because developing red cells use it to build hemoglobin, and research links a limited iron supply during red cell production with smaller cells.[4] Iron stores themselves show up on a different test, ferritin.

How It's Measured

The indices don't need their own blood draw. Analyzers calculate them from the direct measurements on a complete blood count: MCV from hematocrit divided by the red blood cell count, MCH from hemoglobin divided by the red cell count, and MCHC from hemoglobin divided by hematocrit.[3][6] All three land on the same CBC report, and each lab prints its own reference interval next to them.

Why People Track MCV, MCH, and MCHC

The CBC is one of the most common blood tests, and it reports red blood cells, hemoglobin, and the indices in a single panel.[5] So if you've had routine bloodwork, you probably already have MCV, MCH, and MCHC results sitting in a PDF somewhere. People who upload their labs track the indices for the same reason they track hemoglobin or a red cell count: the numbers arrive together and read best side by side over time.

What the Research Shows

Hematology defines MCV as the average size and volume of red blood cells and builds its cell-size vocabulary on it.[1]

Hematology references still define the indices by formulas built on hematocrit, hemoglobin, and red cell count, the same direct measurements automated analyzers report on a CBC.[3][6]

Iron is central to making heme, the oxygen-carrying part of hemoglobin, and research on iron-limited red cell production describes the resulting cells as smaller than typical.[4]

Related Terms

Red Blood Cells

The count behind the indices, reported on the same CBC panel.

Hemoglobin

The oxygen-carrying protein that MCH and MCHC average out per cell.

Hematocrit

The packed red cell volume that analyzers use to calculate MCV.

Ferritin

The iron-storage marker often reviewed alongside red-cell values.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Are MCV, MCH, and MCHC separate blood tests?

No. All three come from a standard complete blood count, calculated from the same draw's hematocrit, hemoglobin, and red blood cell count. Labs report them together as the red cell indices.

What's the difference between MCH and MCHC?

MCH is the average amount of hemoglobin inside a single red blood cell. MCHC is that hemoglobin relative to the cell's size, a concentration rather than an amount. Two cells can carry the same amount of hemoglobin and still differ in MCHC if one cell is bigger than the other.

Does Go Go Gaia tell me what my MCV means?

No. The app stores and charts the values you upload but doesn't interpret them, flag them, or compare them to a range. Your lab report carries its own reference interval, and what your numbers mean is a conversation for your doctor.
