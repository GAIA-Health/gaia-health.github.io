Hot Flashes

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

A hot flash is a sudden wave of heat, usually felt across the face, neck, and chest, often with sweating and flushed skin.[1] Episodes typically last one to five minutes and are linked to changing estrogen levels and altered temperature regulation in the brain, most often during the menopause transition.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What a Hot Flash Is

A hot flash combines a feeling of intense internal heat with profuse sweating and widened blood vessels near the skin.[2] The leading physiological explanation is a narrowed thermoneutral zone, the range of core body temperature the body tolerates before it starts sweating or shivering. In laboratory studies, that zone measured close to zero in women with frequent hot flashes versus about 0.4°C in women without them, so small rises in core temperature that would normally go unnoticed trigger the full sweating-and-flushing response.[2] Falling estrogen contributes, but estrogen levels alone don't explain it. Plasma estrogen doesn't correlate with how severe symptoms are.[2] Hot flashes and night sweats together are called vasomotor symptoms, and roughly 75% to 80% of women experience them at some point during the menopause transition.[1]

How It's Logged

There's no lab test or wearable reading for a hot flash in daily life. It's a logged symptom. You record that one happened and how frequent or intense the day felt, which turns a fleeting episode into a data point you can chart over weeks and months.

Why People Track Hot Flashes

Hot flashes come and go over years, and memory is a poor record of a symptom that lasts minutes. A dated log shows how often episodes actually happen, whether frequency is shifting month to month, and how flash-heavy days line up with other logged metrics like sleep, cycle phase, or mood. During perimenopause, when cycles turn irregular, that pattern over time is often more informative than any single day.

What the Research Shows

The Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) followed 1,449 women with frequent vasomotor symptoms and found a median total duration of 7.4 years. Women whose symptoms started before or early in the menopause transition had the longest course, with a median above 11.8 years, while symptoms that began after the final period lasted a median of 3.4 years.[3]

SWAN analyses also report that 60% to 80% of women experience vasomotor symptoms at some point during the transition, with occurrence peaking in late perimenopause and the early postmenopausal years around the final menstrual period.[4]

Laboratory work shows small but measurable rises in core body temperature precede most hot flashes, supporting the narrowed-thermoneutral-zone model of how they're triggered.[2]

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a single hot flash last?

Research describes individual hot flashes as usually lasting one to five minutes, with some episodes stretching longer. A typical episode involves a sudden feeling of internal heat, sweating, and widened blood vessels near the skin.[2]

How many years do hot flashes typically continue?

In the SWAN study, which followed 1,449 women with frequent vasomotor symptoms, the median total duration was 7.4 years. Women whose symptoms began before or early in the menopause transition reported the longest durations, with a median above 11.8 years.[3]

What causes a hot flash physiologically?

Research points to a narrowed thermoneutral zone, the range of core body temperature the body tolerates before it sweats or shivers. Small rises in core temperature that would normally go unnoticed can then trigger sweating and flushing. Changing estrogen levels contribute, though estrogen levels alone don't fully explain the symptom.[2]
