Breast Tenderness

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Breast tenderness is soreness, heaviness, or aching in the breast tissue. When it follows the menstrual cycle it's called cyclical mastalgia, the most common type of breast pain.[1] It usually builds during the luteal phase, as hormone shifts cause lobule enlargement and stromal edema, and eases with menstruation.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Breast Tenderness Is

Clinicians split breast pain (mastalgia) into two groups: cyclic pain, which has a clear relationship to the menstrual cycle, and noncyclic pain, which doesn't.[2] Cyclic pain is the most common type, experienced by about two-thirds of affected women.[1]

The mechanism is hormonal. Across the cycle, shifts in estrogen, progesterone, and prolactin change the breast tissue itself: estrogen stimulates the ductal elements, and the combined fluctuations drive lobule enlargement, basal epithelial cell proliferation, and stromal edema. That's why tenderness often arrives together with swelling and lumpiness, intensifies in the luteal phase, and subsides once the period starts.[1] Progesterone dominates the luteal phase, the roughly two weeks between ovulation and the next period.[4]

How It's Measured

There's no lab test or device for tenderness. It's a self-rated symptom: you score how sore your breasts feel on a simple daily scale. Logged over a few cycles, the ratings show whether the pain tracks your cycle (cyclic) or ignores it (noncyclic), which is the same timing distinction clinicians use.[2]

Why People Track Breast Tenderness

Breast pain is common. In a survey of 1,659 women from the general population, 51.5% reported it, and among those women 41% said it affected sex and 35% said it affected sleep.[3] Daily logging answers the question memory can't: does the soreness land on the same cycle days every month? A dated record of when it starts and stops is also a concrete thing to bring to a doctor's appointment.

What the Research Shows

Mastalgia affects up to 70% of women at some point, and cyclic pain is the most common form.[1] In the general-population survey above, severity levels were comparable to those seen in clinical patient groups, and higher rates of breast pain went with increasing age, larger breast size, and lower activity levels.[3]

Cyclic pain also often changes on its own: 20% to 30% of women with cyclical pain show spontaneous resolution within 3 months, though around 60% see it recur.[1]

Related Terms

Luteal Phase

The post-ovulation phase where cyclical breast pain typically intensifies before easing with menstruation.

Progesterone

The dominant luteal-phase hormone, one of the fluctuations studied in cyclical mastalgia.

Estradiol

The estrogen that stimulates breast ductal elements across the cycle.

Bloating

Another symptom that clusters around the luteal phase and start of flow in research.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What is cyclical mastalgia?

Cyclical mastalgia is breast pain with a clear relationship to the menstrual cycle.[2] It typically intensifies during the luteal phase, the weeks between ovulation and the next period, and subsides with menstruation. It's the most common type of breast pain, experienced by about two-thirds of affected women.[1]

Why do breasts get sore before a period?

Researchers attribute cyclical breast pain to normal hormonal fluctuations across the cycle. Shifting estrogen, progesterone, and prolactin levels drive changes in breast tissue, including lobule enlargement and stromal edema, which show up as swelling, lumpiness, and soreness that ease when the period starts.[1]

How common is breast pain?

Very common. In a general-population survey of 1,659 women, 51.5% reported breast pain,[3] and StatPearls notes mastalgia affects up to 70% of women at some point.[1]
