Fiber

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Fiber is a non-digestible complex carbohydrate that comes in two forms.[2] Soluble fiber dissolves in water and is partly fermented in the colon, while insoluble fiber doesn't dissolve and mainly adds bulk to stool.[1] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks it in grams from the meals you log.

What Fiber Is

Fiber is the carbohydrate the body can't break down into glucose, and it comes in two structurally different types.[2] Soluble fiber, which includes pectin, gums, mucilage, fructans, and some resistant starches, dissolves in water, forms a gel that slows gastric emptying, delays glucose absorption, and blunts post-meal blood sugar spikes.[1] Insoluble fiber, including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, doesn't dissolve. Instead, it reduces intestinal transit time and increases fecal bulk to promote digestive regularity.[1]

Fiber also feeds the gut. Bacteria in the colon ferment it to produce short-chain fatty acids, which have anti-inflammatory effects that support gut and metabolic health more broadly.[1]

Why People Track Fiber

Because fiber changes stool bulk, transit time, and how a meal moves through the gut, some people log fiber intake alongside logged digestive changes like bloating, constipation, or diarrhea, to see how the two line up over time. It's the same logic as tracking carbohydrates next to glucose data: fiber and digestion sit on the same mechanical chain, so charting them together can surface patterns that a single log entry wouldn't show on its own.

What the Research Shows

Soluble fiber's gel-forming action slows gastric emptying and delays how quickly glucose from a meal reaches the bloodstream, which is one reason fiber and blood glucose data are sometimes tracked together.[1] Since digestion converts most carbohydrate into glucose in the first place, slowing that absorption changes how quickly the meal's fuel becomes available to the body.[3]

Insoluble fiber's main mechanical effects are on transit time and stool bulk rather than blood sugar, which is why soluble and insoluble fiber are typically discussed as having different digestive roles rather than interchangeable ones.[1]

One StatPearls review found that among American adults surveyed between 2017 and 2020, average fiber intake was about 17 grams a day, well under the adequate-intake reference that dietary guidelines use.[1]

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia tell me how much fiber to eat?

No. Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks the grams of fiber in the meals you log and adds it to your nutrition data. The Encyclopedia doesn't publish intake targets or eating advice.

What's the difference between soluble and insoluble fiber?

Soluble fiber dissolves in water, forms a gel that slows gastric emptying, and is partly fermented by gut bacteria in the colon. Insoluble fiber doesn't dissolve and mainly reduces intestinal transit time while adding bulk to stool. Most fiber-containing foods have a mix of both.

Why might fiber intake matter for digestive symptoms?

Fiber changes stool bulk, transit time, and how quickly a meal empties from the stomach, all of which are mechanically tied to bowel patterns. That's why some people log fiber intake alongside logged digestive symptoms, to see how the two line up over time. Go Go Gaia doesn't interpret that relationship for you.
