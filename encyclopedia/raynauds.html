Raynaud's Syndrome

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Raynaud's syndrome (Raynaud's phenomenon) is an episodic narrowing of the small blood vessels in the fingers and toes, usually triggered by cold or emotional stress, that temporarily cuts blood flow to the skin.[1] It's classified as primary, with no underlying disease, or secondary, most often linked to an autoimmune connective tissue disease.[1][3]

What Raynaud's Syndrome Is

During an episode, the sympathetic nervous system triggers a release of norepinephrine and vasoconstricting neuropeptides, which narrows the digital arteries and cutaneous arterioles that supply the fingers and toes.[1] Cold temperatures and emotional stress are the two triggers named most consistently in the research.[1] As blood flow drops and then returns, the skin often moves through a color sequence, pale or white first, sometimes blue, then red.[1]

Research separates Raynaud's into two categories. Primary Raynaud's occurs on its own, with no other disease behind it. Secondary Raynaud's is tied to another condition, and reviews describe it as often the earliest sign of an underlying autoimmune connective tissue disease, systemic sclerosis in particular.[3][2] Published estimates put overall prevalence at roughly 20% to 30% among women, with a female-to-male ratio of about 9 to 1, and the condition shows up most often in younger age groups.[1]

Why People Track It

Because an episode is defined by what the skin does in the moment, the color change itself, pale or white, then sometimes blue, then red, is the detail research and clinical descriptions come back to most.[1] Alongside that, people commonly note what came before an episode: exposure to cold hands or feet or a broader pattern of cold intolerance, since both are named as common triggers and accompanying sensations.[1] Because secondary Raynaud's can be an early marker of autoimmune connective tissue disease, some people also track it alongside other symptoms over time rather than treating a single episode as the full picture.[3]

What the Research Shows

A 2024 review in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology describes cold and emotional stress as the two most consistently reported triggers of a Raynaud's episode, and lays out the vasospastic mechanism behind the color change clinicians look for.[1]

A 2019 review in the British Journal of Hospital Medicine notes that Raynaud's phenomenon is often the earliest manifestation of an underlying autoimmune connective tissue disease, which is why secondary Raynaud's is evaluated differently from the primary form.[2]

Reviews of primary and secondary Raynaud's report that the condition affects roughly 20% to 30% of women, with a female-to-male ratio near 9 to 1, one of the more pronounced sex differences documented among vasospastic conditions.[3]

Related Terms

Cold Hands or Feet

A commonly logged trigger and accompanying sensation around Raynaud's episodes.

Cold Intolerance

A broader sensitivity to cold, distinct from an acute vasospastic episode.

Joint Pain

A symptom sometimes logged alongside secondary Raynaud's and the connective tissue diseases it can signal.

Fatigue

A common, nonspecific symptom logged alongside many autoimmune conditions Raynaud's can accompany.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What is Raynaud's syndrome?

Raynaud's syndrome is an episodic narrowing of the small blood vessels in the fingers and toes, usually triggered by cold or emotional stress. During an episode, blood flow drops sharply, and the skin often changes color, first pale or white, sometimes then blue, then red as blood flow returns.[1]

What's the difference between primary and secondary Raynaud's?

Primary Raynaud's happens on its own, with no underlying disease. Secondary Raynaud's is linked to another condition, most often an autoimmune connective tissue disease such as systemic sclerosis, and research describes it as sometimes the earliest sign of that underlying disease.[1][2]

Is Raynaud's syndrome more common in women?

Yes. Published estimates put Raynaud's prevalence at roughly 20% to 30% among women, with a female-to-male ratio of about 9 to 1, and it's most frequently reported in younger age groups.[1][3]
