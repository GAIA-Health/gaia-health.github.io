Brittle Nails

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Brittle nails are nails that split, peel, or break more easily than usual.[1] Research documents nail involvement in the large majority of hypothyroidism cases and in a smaller share of hyperthyroidism cases, and links koilonychia, a specific spoon-shaped nail change, to iron-deficiency anemia.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Brittle Nails Describe

A 2020 dermatology review on brittle nail syndrome reports that thyroid disease is one of the more strongly documented links: the nails are affected in about 90% of patients with hypothyroidism, typically appearing thin, brittle, slow-growing, and with longitudinal or transverse ridging, while nail changes characterized by brittleness and softness show up in about 5% of hyperthyroidism cases and are often reversible once the underlying condition is treated.[1]

Iron deficiency has a more specific, better-documented nail connection: koilonychia, where the nail plate flattens and curves upward into a spoon shape. MedlinePlus describes koilonychia as associated with iron-deficiency anemia, and a StatPearls chapter on iron-deficiency anemia lists it as a finding in chronic or severe cases.[2] The 2020 dermatology review is more cautious about a broader iron-brittleness link, noting that studies directly correlating iron deficit with general brittle nails, rather than koilonychia specifically, are rarely reported.[1] That's a distinction worth holding onto: the shape change is well documented, general brittleness less so.

How It's Logged

There's no home test for nail brittleness. It's logged as a symptom you notice, recorded as a yes/no or a note for the day.

Why People Track Brittle Nails

Nail changes happen slowly enough that they're easy to notice without remembering exactly when they started. A log makes that timeline concrete. Some people who get thyroid or iron panels drawn chart their nail logs against lab results like TSH or ferritin to see how closely the two move together.

What the Research Shows

The 2020 review, published in Dermatology and Therapy, is the most direct source on brittle nail syndrome and its documented causes, and its thyroid-disease statistics, 90% nail involvement in hypothyroidism and 5% in hyperthyroidism, are among the clearest figures in this area of dermatology research.[1]

Clinical references on iron-deficiency anemia consistently list koilonychia among the physical findings in chronic or severe cases, alongside pallor and pica, though they describe these findings as often subtle rather than universal.[2]

MedlinePlus's overview of nail abnormalities frames brittle nails as commonly age-related but also notes they can result from certain diseases and conditions, listing thyroid disease and iron deficiency among the documented causes worth knowing about.[3]

Related Terms

Hypothyroidism

The condition where nail involvement is documented in around 90% of cases.

Iron-Deficiency Anemia

The condition linked to koilonychia, a specific spoon-shaped nail change.

Ferritin

The iron-storage marker most often tracked alongside iron-deficiency anemia.

Pale Skin (Pallor)

Another physical finding documented alongside nail changes in iron-deficiency anemia.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

How often are nails affected by thyroid disease?

A dermatology review reports the nails are affected in about 90% of people with hypothyroidism, typically appearing thin, brittle, slow-growing, and with ridging, and in about 5% of hyperthyroidism cases, where nails can become brittle and soft.[1]

Does iron deficiency cause brittle nails?

Iron deficiency is specifically linked to koilonychia, a spoon-shaped nail change, in clinical references on iron-deficiency anemia.[2] A dermatology review of brittle nails more broadly notes that studies directly correlating iron deficiency with general nail brittleness are rarely reported, so the koilonychia link is better documented than a broader brittleness link.[1]

How is brittle nails logged if it's a physical sign, not a lab value?

It's recorded as a symptom you notice, not a measured reading. You log the days your nails felt or looked more brittle than usual, which can sit alongside lab results like TSH or ferritin if you have them.
