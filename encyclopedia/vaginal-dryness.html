Vaginal Dryness

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Vaginal dryness is a reduction in the vagina's natural moisture, often felt as itching, irritation, or discomfort during sex.[1] Estrogen keeps vaginal tissue thick, elastic, and lubricated, so dryness is closely tied to falling estrogen, most often during the menopause transition and during postpartum breastfeeding.[1],[3]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Vaginal Dryness Is

Estrogen receptors sit throughout the vulva, vagina, urethra, and bladder, and they respond to estrogen by maintaining blood flow, tissue thickness, elasticity, and moisture in those tissues.[1] When estrogen falls, the vaginal lining gets thinner and less elastic, vaginal pH rises as protective lactobacilli decrease, and lubrication drops off.[1] That combination is what shows up as dryness, along with itching, burning, or pain with sex.

The two life stages where this comes up most are the menopause transition, when ovarian estrogen production declines, and the postpartum period while breastfeeding, when prolactin, the hormone that drives milk production, suppresses the signaling that normally keeps estrogen up.[3] Researchers have proposed calling the postpartum version "genitourinary syndrome of lactation," a deliberate parallel to the same estrogen-driven pattern seen in menopause.[3]

What the Research Shows

A SWAN cohort study followed 2,435 women with questionnaire and hormone data across 17 years of the menopause transition. Reported vaginal dryness rose from 19.4% at baseline, when women were 42 to 53 years old, to 34.0% at the final visit measured, when the same women were 57 to 69.[2] Advancing menopausal stage was associated with developing dryness, and among women not using hormone therapy, higher estradiol was linked to lower odds of dryness.[2]

Postpartum, a prospective cohort of 832 first-time mothers (the MAMMI study) found 43% reported a lack of vaginal lubrication at 6 months after birth, falling to 35.4% at 12 months. Breastfeeding was linked to significantly higher odds of the symptom at 6 months.[4]

The proposed physiological explanation for the postpartum pattern is the same estrogen-suppression mechanism seen in menopause, triggered instead by breastfeeding-driven prolactin.[3]

Related Terms

Estradiol

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is vaginal dryness caused by low estrogen?

Estrogen keeps the vaginal lining thick, elastic, and moist, so falling estrogen is a well-established driver of vaginal dryness.[1] Research also shows the connection isn't purely a matter of estrogen level: in the SWAN cohort, advancing menopausal stage was associated with dryness while estradiol level on its own showed only a weak association, so other factors are involved too.[2]

Why does breastfeeding cause vaginal dryness?

Breastfeeding raises prolactin, the hormone that stimulates milk production, and prolactin suppresses the signaling that drives estrogen production.[3] That temporary drop in estrogen while lactating is linked to vaginal dryness, similar to what happens during the menopause transition.[3]

How common is vaginal dryness during perimenopause?

In a longitudinal study following the same women for years through the menopause transition, reported vaginal dryness rose from about 19% near the start of the transition to 34% in the postmenopausal years that followed.[2]
