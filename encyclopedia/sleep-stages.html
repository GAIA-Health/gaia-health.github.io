Sleep Stages (REM, Deep, Core/Light Sleep)

Biometrics & Vitals · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Sleep stages are the recurring phases your body cycles through overnight: light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep, plus brief periods of wake. A full cycle through these stages takes roughly 90 to 110 minutes, and a typical night includes four to five cycles.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Sleep Stages Measure

Clinical sleep science divides a night into five stages: wake, three non-REM stages (N1, N2, N3), and REM. N1 is the brief drift into sleep. N2 is the largest share of the night, roughly 45% of total sleep time. N3, also called deep sleep or slow-wave sleep, has the slowest brain waves and the highest arousal threshold. REM is the stage most associated with dreaming, marked by fast eye movements and near-total muscle relaxation elsewhere in the body. A night moves through these stages in a repeating order, N1 to N2 to N3 back to N2 then REM, with REM stretches getting longer as the night goes on.[1]

How It's Measured

The clinical gold standard is polysomnography (PSG), an in-lab study that records brain waves, eye movement, and muscle activity directly. Consumer wearables don't measure any of those signals. Instead they infer stage from movement, heart rate, and heart rate variability patterns, then run that data through a scoring algorithm. A 2023 multicenter study comparing 11 consumer sleep trackers, including ring and watch devices, against in-lab polysomnography found agreement varied a lot by device and by stage: some trackers scored wake and REM more reliably, others scored deep sleep more reliably, and none matched PSG across every stage.[2] That gap is worth knowing about before treating any wearable's stage breakdown as a lab-grade reading.

Why People Track Sleep Stages

Sleep-stage data gives a more detailed picture of a night than total time asleep alone, since two nights of equal length can carry very different amounts of deep or REM sleep. People also track it alongside cycle data because sleep architecture is one of the things hormonal shifts are hypothesized to touch, alongside body temperature and heart rate. The research on that link is genuinely mixed, covered below.

What the Research Shows

The evidence on whether sleep architecture shifts across the menstrual cycle disagrees, and that disagreement is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. A polysomnography study tracking women across the follicular and luteal phases found reduced REM sleep in the mid-luteal phase compared to the mid-follicular phase, with the effect strongest at certain times of day, pointing to an interaction between the body's circadian rhythm and the menstrual cycle in how REM sleep is regulated.[3]

Against that, a study using Oura Ring data across full cycles in 26 healthy women found no significant change in sleep continuity or sleep stages tied to cycle phase, even as the same device picked up clear luteal-phase increases in heart rate and skin temperature in the same participants.[4] A larger follow-up study across young and midlife participants replicated that null result for sleep specifically, again alongside detectable cycle-phase changes in temperature and heart rate.[5] Two different measurement approaches, two different answers. Neither cancels the other out; they're both real, published findings that simply don't agree.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What's the difference between deep sleep and REM sleep?

Deep sleep (N3) is the stage with the slowest brain waves and the least responsiveness to outside noise. REM sleep is a lighter, more brain-active stage where most dreaming happens, marked by rapid eye movements and near-total muscle relaxation. They're both non-negotiable parts of a normal night, but they involve different brain and body states, and each cycle includes some of both.

How accurate is my wearable's sleep stage data?

It varies by device and by stage. A 2023 multicenter study comparing 11 consumer sleep trackers against in-lab polysomnography found agreement ranged widely, with some devices scoring wake and REM more reliably and others scoring deep sleep more reliably. No consumer wearable matches a lab sleep study exactly, since none directly measures brain waves the way polysomnography does.

Does sleep architecture change across the menstrual cycle?

The evidence is mixed. One polysomnography study found reduced REM sleep in the luteal phase compared to the follicular phase. A separate study using Oura Ring data across full cycles found no significant change in sleep stages by cycle phase, a finding a later study replicated. Both types of studies are real and published. They just don't agree.
