Calories (Dietary Energy)

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Calories, logged in the app as Dietary Energy, are the kilocalories your food logger calculates from every meal you log. A kilocalorie is the amount of energy needed to raise one kilogram of water by one degree Celsius,[1] and food-energy values are built from fixed per-gram factors for protein, carbohydrate, fat, and alcohol.[2]

What Calories Measure

A calorie is a unit of energy, not a nutrient. In biochemistry, a "small" calorie is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one gram of water by one degree Celsius, while the "big" calorie, the kilocalorie or Calorie that shows up on a food label, is a thousand times larger and is the energy needed to raise one kilogram of water by that same degree.[1] Every food's total energy is built from its macronutrients, not measured as one single thing.

The standard method for calculating that total uses the Atwater general factor system, which multiplies a food's grams of protein, carbohydrate, fat, and alcohol by fixed conversion factors, 4 kilocalories per gram for protein and carbohydrate, 9 for fat, and 7 for alcohol, then sums the results.[2] Developed by W.O. Atwater at the USDA in the late nineteenth century, this same-factor-for-every-food approach is still widely used today because of how simple it is to apply, even though it's a modeled estimate rather than a direct measurement of any individual food.[2]

How It's Logged

Dietary Energy is a per-meal, per-kilocalorie value, not a lab result or a biometric. Go Go Gaia's food logger calculates it from the macronutrient breakdown of each meal you log and rolls it into your daily nutrition total, separate from Active Energy, the calories your watch or phone estimates you burned through movement.

Why People Track Dietary Energy

Logged energy intake is one input in a fuller nutrition picture, alongside protein, carbohydrates, and dietary fat. It's also a value that isn't flat across the month for most people. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 15 datasets and 330 female participants found average daily energy intake was significantly higher in the luteal phase than the follicular phase, by a crude average of 168 kilocalories a day.[3] That's a population-level research finding about intake patterns across the cycle, not a target or a recommendation for any individual.

What the Research Shows

The kilocalorie itself is a fixed physical unit: the energy needed to raise one kilogram of water by one degree Celsius, a thousand times the "small" calorie used in basic chemistry.[1]

Food energy values are modeled, not weighed directly. The Atwater general factor system applies the same conversion factor to a nutrient regardless of which food it's found in, 4 kilocalories per gram for protein and carbohydrate and 9 for fat, an approach developed over a century ago that remains the basis for most food energy values today, including the ones USDA food databases report.[2]

On the cycle-intake research, the 2024 meta-analysis found a statistically significant pooled difference between phases (standardized mean difference 0.69, P = .039), though the picture varied by study: 8 of the 15 datasets found a statistically significant luteal-phase increase, averaging about 275 kilocalories a day, the rest found no significant difference between phases, and one study actually found higher intake in the follicular phase.[3]

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia tell me how many calories to eat?

No. This page defines Dietary Energy as a unit of measurement, the kilocalories your food logger calculates from what you log. It doesn't publish intake targets, deficit or surplus numbers, or weight-management advice.

What's the difference between Dietary Energy and Active Energy in the app?

Dietary Energy is the kilocalories your food logger calculates from the meals you log, an intake value. Active Energy is a separate value synced from Apple HealthKit, reflecting energy your watch or phone estimates you burned through movement. Go Go Gaia tracks and charts them as two distinct data types.

Does logged calorie intake really change across the menstrual cycle?

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 15 datasets and 330 participants found average daily energy intake was about 168 kilocalories higher in the luteal phase than the follicular phase, a statistically significant difference. It's a population-level research finding about intake patterns, not a target or recommendation.
