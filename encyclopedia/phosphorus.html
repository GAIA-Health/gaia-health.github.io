Phosphorus (Serum)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Phosphorus is the second most abundant mineral in the body, with about 85% of it stored in bone and teeth alongside calcium.[2] The rest works inside cells, forming part of ATP, DNA, RNA, and cell membranes.[2] On a lab panel it's measured as serum phosphate from a standard blood draw.

What Phosphorus Measures

Phosphorus is the second most abundant mineral in the body, after calcium. About 85% of it is stored in bone, mineralized alongside calcium as hydroxyapatite, the calcium-phosphate crystal that gives bone its rigidity.[2][4] The remaining share, a small fraction of the total, works inside cells and in blood.[2]

That intracellular share does a lot despite its size. Phosphorus is a structural part of DNA, RNA, and cell membranes, and it's essential to how cells make and use ATP, the molecule that powers most cellular energy transfer.[2] Three hormones regulate how much phosphate the body absorbs, holds onto, or excretes: vitamin D increases absorption in the gut, parathyroid hormone increases how much the kidneys excrete while also boosting gut absorption, and FGF-23 decreases both.[2]

How It's Measured

Phosphorus is measured from a standard blood draw and reported as serum phosphate. It's commonly ordered alongside calcium, since the two minerals are metabolically linked, and alongside kidney function tests like creatinine and BUN, since the kidneys handle most of the body's phosphate filtering and reabsorption.[2] Each lab sets and reports its own reference interval with the result.

Why People Track Phosphorus

Phosphorus is easy to get from food. In a national dietary intake analysis, dairy products supplied the largest single share of Americans' daily phosphorus intake, followed by breads and bakery products, with vegetables, chicken, Mexican dishes, and pizza rounding out the rest.[1] Other common sources include meat, poultry, and peanuts.[3]

Because so much of the body's phosphorus is locked into bone alongside calcium, the two minerals get tracked as a pair more often than either is tracked alone. Clinical reference literature ties a long-standing shortfall in phosphorus intake to weakened bone structure over time, the same category of problem, like osteopenia and osteomalacia, that comes up in calcium-deficiency research.[3]

What the Research Shows

Phosphorus physiology research describes about 85% of the body's phosphorus as stored in bone and teeth, with the rest distributed across cells and extracellular fluid.[2]

A national dietary intake analysis found dairy products were the top contributor to Americans' daily phosphorus intake, at roughly a fifth of the day's total, ahead of breads and bakery products.[1]

Bone remodeling research describes bone matrix as an inorganic portion of calcium-phosphate crystals, or hydroxyapatite, built by osteoblasts and broken back down by osteoclasts as part of ordinary bone turnover.[4] Clinical reference literature ties long-term phosphorus deficiency to weakened bone structure, including osteopenia, osteoporosis, and, in more severe or prolonged cases, rickets or osteomalacia.[3]

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia run phosphorus blood tests?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't draw blood or run labs. You get a phosphorus result from a blood draw ordered through a clinician or lab service, then upload it to your account so it's stored and charted alongside your other tracked data.

What's the difference between dietary phosphorus and serum phosphorus?

Dietary phosphorus is what you take in from food, tracked in Go Go Gaia's food logger when you log a meal. Serum phosphorus is the amount circulating in blood, regulated by hormones like vitamin D, parathyroid hormone, and FGF-23 rather than a direct readout of what you ate. The app treats them as separate kinds of data: one from your food log, one from an uploaded lab result.

Why are calcium and phosphorus usually tested together?

The two minerals are metabolically linked. Most of the body's phosphorus is stored in bone mineralized alongside calcium as hydroxyapatite, and shared hormones, including parathyroid hormone and vitamin D, regulate both. Labs often order them as a pair, along with kidney function tests, since the kidneys manage how much of each gets filtered and reabsorbed.
