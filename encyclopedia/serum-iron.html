Serum Iron

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Serum iron measures the iron circulating in your blood at the moment of the draw, nearly all of it bound to a transport protein called transferrin.[1] It's one of four tests in a standard iron panel, read alongside ferritin, TIBC, and transferrin saturation rather than on its own.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Serum Iron Measures

Iron doesn't float free in blood. Once absorbed, it's bound to transferrin, the protein that holds iron in a soluble form, keeps it from generating reactive oxygen species, and ferries it into cells that need it.[1] Serum iron counts that circulating, transferrin-bound iron at the moment of the draw. It's a snapshot of iron in transit, not the much larger amount held in storage. Stored iron is what a ferritin test reflects.

How It's Measured

Serum iron comes from a standard venous blood draw and is reported in micrograms per deciliter. It's usually ordered as part of an iron panel with total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) and transferrin saturation, plus ferritin, because each test describes a different part of how the body carries and stores iron.[2] Transferrin saturation is even calculated from it directly: serum iron divided by TIBC.[3] Labs report their own reference interval with each result.

Why Serum Iron Swings Within a Day

Serum iron is the moody member of the iron panel. Published references describe within-day swings of as much as 30%, and day-to-day variation of roughly 25% to 30% in the same person.[3] The pattern isn't even predictable. When researchers sampled healthy adults at morning, noon, and afternoon, the swings were real but morning values beat afternoon values for only about half of the participants.[4]

Recent meals move it too. In a study that fed participants test meals with a known amount of labeled iron, serum iron rose afterward, and the size of the rise closely tracked how much iron was absorbed.[5] That's normal physiology, not a flaw in the test. It's also why serum iron is read next to steadier companions like ferritin and TIBC instead of standing alone.

What the Research Shows

Transferrin's role as the blood's iron carrier is settled biochemistry: it chelates iron, keeps it soluble, and delivers it to cells, which is what makes serum iron a measure of iron in transit.[1]

A 2002 study that sampled 20 healthy adults across the day found real within-day variation in serum iron but no consistent diurnal pattern, and within-day variation matched day-to-day variation.[4]

The rise in serum iron after an iron-containing test meal correlated strongly with measured whole-body iron absorption, direct evidence that recent intake shows up in the number.[5]

Related Terms

Ferritin

The storage side of the iron story: a marker of the iron your body holds in reserve.

TIBC & Transferrin Saturation

How much iron-carrying capacity the blood has, and how much of it is in use.

The iron-containing protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Why does serum iron change so much between blood draws?

Serum iron is a snapshot of iron in transit, not a measure of stored iron, so it moves with normal physiology. Published references describe swings of as much as 30% within a day, day-to-day variation of roughly 25% to 30%, and a rise after an iron-containing meal. That's why labs read it alongside ferritin, TIBC, and transferrin saturation instead of on its own.

What's the difference between serum iron and ferritin?

Serum iron measures the iron circulating in blood at the moment of the draw, almost all of it bound to transferrin. Ferritin reflects the iron the body holds in storage inside cells. One is a moment-to-moment snapshot and the other is a marker of reserves, which is why the two tests are usually ordered and read together.
