Pelvic Pain

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Pelvic pain is discomfort felt in the lower abdomen, below the belly button, or in the pelvis and lower back.[1] It's a broader category than menstrual cramps: cramps are cyclic pain that arrives with a period, while pelvic pain can occur at any cycle day, on one side or both, and can persist or come and go outside of menstruation.[4]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Pelvic Pain Is

Clinicians generally split pelvic pain into two patterns. Cyclic pain follows the menstrual cycle and includes ordinary period cramps. Pain that's persistent or recurs for six months or more, cycle-linked or not, falls under a separate clinical definition, chronic pelvic pain, especially when it's also affecting daily function, mood, or sex.[1][4] Location matters too. Pain can sit centrally in the lower abdomen or lateralize to one side, and it can show up near ovulation, before a period, during sex, with a bowel movement, or without any clear trigger at all.

Mid-cycle, one-sided pain around ovulation, sometimes called mittelschmerz, is one well-studied example of how location and timing carry information. Research describes it occurring on the same side as the ovary releasing an egg that cycle, sometimes switching sides the next month and sometimes staying put, and ties the sensation to a prostaglandin-driven contraction of tissue around the follicle as LH surges.[2] A single episode usually resolves within three to twelve hours.[2]

How It's Logged

There's no scan or blood test for pelvic pain itself. It's a self-reported symptom. A useful entry notes the date, where the pain sits (left, right, or center), how it relates to cycle day, and anything that seemed to trigger or ease it. Logged this way over months, isolated days turn into a pattern: whether pain clusters around ovulation, the days before a period, or shows up independent of the cycle altogether.

Why People Track Pelvic Pain

Cramps and pelvic pain get lumped together in casual conversation, but research instruments built to study pelvic pain conditions like endometriosis track them as separate dimensions: menstrual pain, non-cycle pelvic pain, and pain with sex are each logged and scored on their own.[3] Splitting them out in a personal log mirrors that distinction, since pain that lines up with ovulation, pain that's unrelated to the cycle, and pain tied to a specific activity are each different pieces of information.

Same-day logging is also more accurate than reconstructing weeks of pain from memory. Guidance for patient-reported pain measures calls for a recall window close to when the pain happened, because retrospective recall drifts the further back it reaches.[3] A dated log builds that closer-to-real-time record automatically, which is the kind of detail worth bringing into a conversation with a clinician, since a detailed symptom history is a core part of how pelvic pain gets evaluated.[4] For more on how pelvic pain fits into evaluation for a specific underlying condition, see our endometriosis guide.

What the Research Shows

Estimates of how many women experience chronic pelvic pain vary by definition and population, ranging from about 4% to 16% across different reviews, with U.S. estimates commonly cited around 1 in 7 women.[1]

Mittelschmerz is common: it's reported to affect over 40% of women of reproductive age at some point.[2]

Pelvic pain is one of the symptoms researchers study closely in conditions like endometriosis, where a review of 17 studies found the reported time from first symptoms to diagnosis has ranged from under a year to about 12 years across different study populations and definitions.[5] That kind of gap is part of why a detailed, dated symptom history is emphasized in pelvic pain evaluation guidance.[4]

Related Terms

Cramps (Dysmenorrhea)

Crampy pain that follows the start of a period, driven by uterine prostaglandins.

Ovulation

The release of an egg partway through the cycle, one of the events pelvic pain can cluster around.

Spotting (Intermenstrual Bleeding)

Light bleeding between periods, another symptom worth its own dated entry.

Bloating

Abdominal fullness or swelling that often overlaps with pelvic pain around the same cycle days.

Period Flow

The day-by-day record of menstrual bleeding that pelvic pain entries sit alongside.

Cyclical Low Back Pain

Lower-back pain tied to the menstrual cycle that can radiate from the same pelvic region.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

How is pelvic pain different from menstrual cramps?

Menstrual cramps are cyclic pain that arrives with a period. Pelvic pain is the broader category: it can happen at any point in the cycle, on one side or both, and clinical definitions treat persistent or recurring pain lasting six months or more as a separate pattern from ordinary cramps.[1][4]

What causes one-sided pain around ovulation?

This is sometimes called mittelschmerz. Research ties it to a prostaglandin-driven contraction of tissue around the ovarian follicle as LH surges, felt on the same side as the ovary releasing an egg that cycle, and usually resolving within three to twelve hours.[2]

Why log the location and timing of pelvic pain over time?

Research instruments used to study pelvic pain conditions track menstrual pain, non-cycle pain, and pain during sex as separate dimensions, because they carry different information.[3] A dated log captures pain closer to when it happens instead of relying on memory weeks later, and a detailed symptom history is a core part of how pelvic pain gets evaluated.[3][4]
