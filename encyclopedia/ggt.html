GGT (Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) is an enzyme concentrated in the liver, kidney, and pancreas that helps cells recycle the antioxidant glutathione.[1] Because liver and bile-duct cells hold a lot of it, a blood GGT level rises when those tissues are stressed, and clinicians use it to help interpret an elevated alkaline phosphatase result.[2]

What GGT Measures

GGT's job is breaking a specific chemical bond in glutathione, the cell's main internal antioxidant, and passing the freed piece to an amino acid or peptide. That reaction lets a cell recycle the building blocks it needs to make more glutathione, which is part of how the body defends itself against oxidative stress and clears out toxins.[3] The enzyme is highly represented in the kidney, liver, and brain, and unlike ALT and AST, which sit inside liver cells, GGT is anchored to the outside of the cell membrane, with its active site facing out.[3] Because so much circulating GGT comes from the liver, a blood test mainly reads what's happening there.[3]

How It's Measured

GGT is measured from a standard venous blood draw and reported in units per liter (U/L). It's usually ordered alongside the rest of a liver panel, next to ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin, rather than as a stand-alone test.[1],[4] GGT's specific job on that panel is localizing an elevated alkaline phosphatase result. ALP comes from both liver and bone, but GGT isn't made by bone, so a GGT level that rises along with ALP points toward a liver or bile-duct source, while a normal GGT alongside high ALP points elsewhere.[2] Each lab reports its own reference interval with the result.

Why People Track GGT

GGT is also used to screen for or monitor alcohol use, since alcohol is one of several factors known to raise it.[1] Medications and other conditions can raise it too, and researchers describe GGT as easily induced by these factors, which limits how much a single elevated result can say on its own.[2] That's exactly why it's read alongside the rest of a liver panel instead of by itself, and why a dated series that includes GGT next to ALT, AST, and alkaline phosphatase carries more information than any one value alone.

What the Research Shows

GGT's role in glutathione metabolism is well established: it catalyzes cleavage of the gamma-glutamyl bond in glutathione and transfers the released group to amino acids or peptides, a reaction that helps cells maintain their internal antioxidant defenses.[3]

Clinical references describe GGT and, where available, 5'-nucleotidase as the standard way to localize an elevated alkaline phosphatase result to the hepatobiliary system rather than bone.[2]

GGT is also flagged in the research literature as an imperfect marker on its own, since alcohol, medications, and other conditions can induce it independent of liver injury, which is why it remains most useful when interpreted alongside the rest of a liver panel.[2]

Related Terms

ALT

A liver-cell enzyme reported on the same panel, unlike membrane-bound GGT.

AST

Another core liver-panel enzyme, found in the liver and several other tissues.

Alkaline Phosphatase

The liver-and-bone enzyme GGT is measured alongside to localize its source.

Bilirubin

The pigment value rounding out the standard liver panel GGT appears on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia run GGT blood tests?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't draw blood or run labs. You get a GGT result from a blood draw ordered through a clinician or lab service, then upload it to your Go Go Gaia account so it's stored and charted alongside your other tracked data.

Why is GGT ordered alongside alkaline phosphatase?

Alkaline phosphatase comes from both the liver and bone, so an elevated result on its own doesn't say which one. GGT is made in liver and bile-duct tissue but not in bone, so pairing the two helps place an ALP change in the hepatobiliary system rather than the skeleton.

Can factors other than liver disease raise GGT?

Yes. Alcohol intake and certain medications are known to raise GGT, and researchers describe the enzyme as easily induced by these factors. That's why GGT is read in the context of the rest of a liver panel rather than as a stand-alone number.
