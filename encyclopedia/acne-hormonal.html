Hormonal Acne

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Hormonal acne is acne that tracks with hormone shifts. Sebaceous glands in the skin carry androgen receptors, and androgens stimulate the glands' growth and oil output, which sets up the clogged, inflamed follicles behind breakouts.[1] Many women report their acne flares before their period.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Hormonal Acne Reflects

Acne comes from four things working together: extra sebum (skin oil), clogged follicles, the skin bacterium Cutibacterium acnes, and inflammation.[1] The hormonal part enters through the sebaceous gland. These glands carry androgen receptors that bind testosterone and the more potent DHT, and androgens push the glands to grow and make more oil. The glands can even make androgens locally, converting DHEAS into testosterone with their own enzymes.[1] Glands can also be extra sensitive to androgens, so acne doesn't require raised hormone levels in the blood.[1]

How It's Measured

There's no lab test for a breakout. Acne is a logged observation: you rate how your skin looks on a given day and note where it flares. Rated consistently over a few cycles, those logs show whether your breakouts cluster at a particular cycle phase or follow other patterns.

Why People Track Hormonal Acne

A breakout on its own tells you little. The same breakout dated and charted against your cycle can show a repeating pattern, and cyclical flares are common enough at the population level that they're worth checking for in your own data.[2] Acne also runs in the same androgen family as hirsutism and hair thinning, so people often log them together next to labs like testosterone and DHEA-S.

What the Research Shows

Premenstrual flares are a real population-level pattern. In a survey of 400 women, 44% reported their acne got worse in the days before their period, and the study suggested flares may be more common in women over 33 than in those aged 20 to 33.[2]

Adult acne is common in women. In a survey of 1,013 adults, about 51% of women in their 20s reported acne, along with 35% in their 30s, 26% in their 40s, and 15% at 50 and older. Women reported acne more often than men in every adult age group.[3]

It doesn't reliably end with the teenage years either. An epidemiology review found acne persists into the 20s in about 64% of people and into the 30s in about 43%.[4]

Related Terms

Testosterone

An androgen that binds receptors in sebaceous glands and stimulates oil production.

DHEA-S

The adrenal androgen precursor that sebaceous glands can convert into testosterone locally.

Hirsutism

Androgen-driven terminal-hair growth, the hair-follicle counterpart to androgen-responsive glands.

Hair Thinning

Reduced scalp hair density, another symptom people log alongside androgen labs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Why does acne respond to hormones?

Sebaceous glands carry androgen receptors that bind testosterone and the more potent DHT, and androgens stimulate the glands' growth and oil production. The glands can even make androgens locally from DHEAS. More oil, plus clogged follicles, bacteria, and inflammation, is the pathway behind breakouts.[1]

When in the cycle does acne tend to flare?

In a survey of 400 women, 44% reported their acne got worse in the days before their period.[2] That's a population-level pattern, and individual timing varies, which is one reason people log breakouts against their own cycle.

How common is acne in adult women?

Common. In a survey of over 1,000 adults, about 51% of women in their 20s, 35% in their 30s, 26% in their 40s, and 15% at 50 and older reported acne, with women affected more often than men in every adult age group.[3]
