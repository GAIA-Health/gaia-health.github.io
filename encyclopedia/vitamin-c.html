Vitamin C

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Vitamin C, or L-ascorbic acid, is an essential water-soluble vitamin the body can't make on its own.[1] It plays a central role in collagen synthesis, works as an antioxidant, and helps the body absorb iron from food.[1] Because it isn't stored in any meaningful amount, regular dietary intake is required.[1] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks it from the meals you log.

What Vitamin C Does

Vitamin C is an essential water-soluble vitamin that the body can't produce on its own, so it depends on diet for its central roles in collagen synthesis, antioxidant defense, immune modulation, and iron absorption.[1] As an antioxidant, it counteracts free radicals generated during normal metabolic processes, and it supports the immune system by helping the body produce immunoglobulins and interferons.[1]

Its effect on iron is a practical one. Vitamin C improves how well the body absorbs dietary iron, and it also helps stabilize vitamin E and folic acid.[1] That's part of why iron and vitamin C so often come up in the same conversation, particularly around plant-based iron sources the body absorbs less efficiently on their own.

How It's Logged

Go Go Gaia's food logger breaks the meals you log down into their nutrients, including vitamin C in milligrams, so your intake from foods like citrus fruits, strawberries, tomatoes, potatoes, and green leafy vegetables shows up alongside the rest of your nutrition data.[1] Because the body doesn't store excess vitamin C, it has to be replenished regularly, which is part of why a running log over time tells a different story than any single day's total.[1]

Why Vitamin C Also Shows Up on Lab Panels

Vitamin C turns up somewhere less expected than a meal log: on a urinalysis lab panel, listed as urine ascorbic acid. That's not a dietary measurement. Ascorbic acid in urine is a well-documented interferent in standard dipstick urinalysis, capable of producing false-negative readings for glucose, blood, nitrite, and bilirubin when concentrations run high.[2] Labs include it as a check value for that reason, and it's one of the values Go Go Gaia's lab-upload feature stores if a urinalysis result you upload lists it, kept separate from the dietary vitamin C your food logger tracks.

What the Research Shows

Vitamin C's core functions are well established: it's essential for collagen synthesis, acts as an antioxidant against free radicals generated during metabolism, and supports immune function by helping the body produce immunoglobulins and interferons.[1]

Its effect on iron absorption is documented too. Vitamin C improves dietary iron absorption and helps stabilize both vitamin E and folic acid in the body.[1]

A 2015 study in Annals of Clinical & Laboratory Science had 33 volunteers take 1,000 mg of vitamin C and then tested their urine on dipstick strips. All 33 tested positive for urinary vitamin C, and the researchers found it caused false-negative readings for glucose and blood cells, with interference increasing at higher urinary concentrations.[2] That's the mechanism behind why ascorbic acid gets checked on some urinalysis panels.
