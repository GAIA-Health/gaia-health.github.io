B Vitamins

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

The B vitamins are a family of water-soluble nutrients that act as coenzymes in energy metabolism.[2] Five of them, thiamin (B1), riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3), pantothenic acid (B5), and biotin (B7), are grouped together here.[1] Vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate each have their own page. What the B Vitamins Do

Thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, and biotin belong to the water-soluble B-vitamin family alongside vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate. As a group, B vitamins act as coenzymes in the enzymatic processes that support nearly every aspect of cellular function, though each one plays its own distinct role.[2]

Thiamin (B1) works in the body as thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), a cofactor for enzymes including pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, transketolase, and branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase.[1] TPP sits at crucial steps in the citric acid cycle and pentose phosphate pathway, playing a major role in how the body metabolizes glucose for energy.[2]

Riboflavin (B2) is a cofactor in redox reactions through two active forms, FAD and FMN, that shuttle electrons during cellular energy production.[1] Those active forms are also needed to synthesize niacin, folate, vitamin B6, and heme proteins, and riboflavin itself is involved in metabolizing carbohydrate, protein, and fat.[2] Niacin (B3) functions through NAD+ and NADP+, a related pair of redox coenzymes, and the body can synthesize some niacin from dietary tryptophan.[1]

Pantothenic acid (B5) is a building block of coenzyme A and fatty acid synthase, and it's essential for synthesizing cholesterol, fatty acids, and acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter involved in muscle activation and memory.[1][2] Biotin (B7) is necessary for the metabolism of protein, fat, and carbohydrates, catalyzing reactions across fatty acid, glucose, and amino acid metabolism.[1][2]

What the Research Shows

The five vitamins' coenzyme roles are documented across two independent lines of research: a StatPearls biochemistry review covering the water-soluble vitamins as a class, and a 2022 review in The Permanente Journal focused on B-vitamin functions in medicine.[1][2] Both describe niacin's and riboflavin's active forms as central to redox chemistry, the electron-transfer reactions cells depend on for energy production.[1][2]

Riboflavin's role turns out to be interconnected with the other B vitamins: its active forms are needed to synthesize niacin, folate, vitamin B6, and heme proteins, so its chemistry supports work the other B vitamins ultimately depend on too.[2]

Niacin's NAD+ and NADP+ coenzymes extend past energy metabolism into DNA repair and cholesterol synthesis, and pantothenic acid's role in coenzyme A synthesis extends to building cholesterol, fatty acids, and acetylcholine.[2]

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia track thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, biotin, and pantothenic acid separately?

Yes. Go Go Gaia's food logger breaks each of the five out individually from the meals you log, rather than combining them into one bundled B-complex total, so each one is charted in your nutrition data over time.

Why do these five B vitamins share one page while B6, B12, and folate have their own?

Vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate each come up individually in more distinct contexts, B6 in PMS-related research and B12 and folate in DNA synthesis and red blood cell formation, so they're covered on their own pages. Thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, and biotin share this page because each entry on its own would be comparatively thin.

What do the B vitamins have in common?

All of them are water-soluble and act as coenzymes that support enzymatic reactions across energy and macronutrient metabolism. Because they're water-soluble, the body relies on regular dietary intake rather than a large stored reserve.
