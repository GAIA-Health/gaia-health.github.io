C-Peptide

Hormones & Labs · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

C-peptide is a short protein chain that the pancreas releases alongside insulin. When beta cells make insulin, they split a precursor called proinsulin into one insulin molecule and one C-peptide molecule, so the two enter the blood in equal amounts. A C-peptide test measures it from a lab blood draw.[1]

What C-Peptide Measures

C-peptide starts as part of a bigger molecule. Beta cells in the pancreas first build proinsulin, a single chain that enzymes then cut into two pieces, insulin and C-peptide.[2] The cell stores both in the same secretory vesicles and releases them together, so every insulin molecule that leaves the pancreas comes with one C-peptide molecule.[1] That one-to-one ratio is the whole reason the test exists. Count the C-peptide in a blood sample and you get a read on how much insulin the pancreas made. The insulin entry covers what insulin itself does once it's circulating.

How It's Measured

C-peptide is a lab test. A phlebotomist draws a venous blood sample, often after a fast, and the lab reports the result, commonly in ng/mL.[4] There's no home device for it and no sensation that maps to it. The number only comes from a blood draw.

Why People Track C-Peptide

C-peptide fixes three problems that insulin creates as a measurement. Insulin lasts only 3 to 5 minutes in circulation, while C-peptide lasts 20 to 30, so its level is steadier and easier to catch.[2] The liver removes about half of the insulin the pancreas secretes on the first pass, while C-peptide passes through with negligible hepatic clearance.[2] And in people who inject insulin, standard insulin assays pick up the injected hormone too, while C-peptide reflects only what the pancreas produced.[3]

Together those properties make C-peptide a widely used research marker for the body's own insulin production and beta-cell function.[1] It shows up on metabolic lab panels alongside glucose and insulin, and clinicians sometimes measure it to describe how much insulin a person's pancreas is making when the type of diabetes is unclear.[3]

What the Research Shows

Because the body clears it more slowly, C-peptide circulates at concentrations roughly five times higher than insulin.[3] The kidneys remove about half of it, mostly by breaking it down in the tissue around the renal tubules.[3]

The equal-amounts release is well established. Insulin and C-peptide leave the beta cell in equimolar amounts, which lets researchers use C-peptide concentrations to assess beta-cell function across study populations.[1],[2]

Related Terms

Insulin

The hormone C-peptide is released with, and the one that moves glucose into cells.

Blood Glucose

The sugar the whole insulin system regulates, measured by fingerstick or CGM.

Fasting Glucose

The baseline glucose value that sits next to C-peptide on metabolic panels.

HbA1c

A blood marker that reflects average glucose over the past few months.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

How is a C-peptide test different from an insulin test?

Both come from a blood draw, but they answer different questions. An insulin assay measures all the insulin in the sample, including any that was injected. A C-peptide assay picks up only the peptide the pancreas released alongside its own insulin, so it reflects the body's own production.

Why does C-peptide show the body's own insulin production?

The pancreas splits proinsulin into one insulin molecule and one C-peptide molecule, so the two enter the blood in equal amounts. Injected insulin contains no C-peptide. That makes the C-peptide level a marker of what the pancreas itself made.
