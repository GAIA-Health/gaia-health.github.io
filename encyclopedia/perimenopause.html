Perimenopause

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Perimenopause is the menopausal transition: the stretch of years leading up to menopause when ovarian hormone production becomes irregular before eventually declining.[1] Cycles typically become variable by seven days or more before periods stop altogether, and researchers stage the transition using frameworks like STRAW+10, based on menstrual and hormone markers rather than a fixed age.[1],[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Perimenopause Is

Perimenopause is the medical term for the menopausal transition, the years of hormonal change leading up to menopause itself. Menopause is defined as 12 months without a period, caused by declining ovarian function, and perimenopause covers everything that happens on the way there.[1] Research on this life stage describes it as a natural, physiological transition rather than a disease, even though the hormonal changes it involves can meaningfully affect daily life.[1] The earliest change researchers point to is cycle variability: cycle length starts shifting by seven days or more from one cycle to the next as ovarian hormone production becomes less predictable, well before periods stop entirely.[1]

How Researchers Stage It

Perimenopause isn't diagnosed from a single symptom or test. The framework researchers and clinicians use most, called STRAW+10, divides reproductive aging into three broad phases, the reproductive years, the menopausal transition, and postmenopause, further split into numbered stages and substages.[2] Stages are defined using menstrual bleeding patterns together with hormone and ultrasound markers, including FSH, AMH, and antral follicle count, evaluated over time rather than by age or how a given day feels.[2] The system exists so researchers can compare studies of midlife women on the same terms. It's a research and clinical framework, not a checklist for reading your own stage off a symptom list.

Why People Track Perimenopause

Because perimenopause is defined by change rather than a fixed set of numbers, its signature is a pattern over months and years, not a single day. Research on the transition describes an increase in commonly reported symptoms as women move from early to late perimenopause, including hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, and vaginal dryness.[3] Irregular periods and cognitive complaints sometimes called brain fog track alongside them, with clinical reviews reporting psychogenic symptoms, including trouble concentrating, in up to 70% of women during perimenopause and menopause.[1] None of these show up on a lab report. They're logged symptoms, recorded the same way as any other, and a dated record is what turns a vague sense that things have felt different lately into a visible pattern: which symptoms cluster together, whether they're becoming more frequent, and how they line up with a cycle that's no longer predictable.

What the Research Shows

A review of longitudinal cohort studies on the menopausal transition found menopausal symptoms are common enough to drive almost 90% of women to seek care from a healthcare provider, with hot flashes, the most classic symptom, moderately to severely bothersome for about a third of women.[3]

SWAN analyses report that 60% to 80% of women experience vasomotor symptoms, hot flashes and night sweats, at some point during the transition, with occurrence peaking in late perimenopause and the early postmenopausal years.[4]

A systematic review and meta-analysis of cognition across the menopause transition found verbal memory and verbal fluency differed by menopause stage, with postmenopausal women showing the largest differences, alongside a higher rate of depressive symptoms in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women.[5]

Related Terms

Hot Flashes

A classic vasomotor symptom that increases in frequency across the menopause transition.

Night Sweats

The sleep-time form of vasomotor symptoms, sharing the same underlying physiology as hot flashes.

Irregular Periods

Cycle-length variability, the earliest and most defining change of the perimenopausal years.

Brain Fog

Cognitive complaints linked most consistently to the menopause transition in the research.

Vaginal Dryness

A symptom tied to falling estrogen that tends to persist longer than others without intervention.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is perimenopause a diagnosis or a stage of life?

It's a stage of life, not a diagnosis. Research describes it as a natural, physiological transition tied to declining ovarian function rather than a disease process, even though the hormonal changes involved can bring noticeable symptoms.[1]

How do researchers know someone has entered perimenopause?

There's no single test. The STRAW+10 framework researchers use most often stages the transition through menstrual bleeding patterns together with hormone and ultrasound markers like FSH, AMH, and antral follicle count, evaluated over time rather than from one reading.[2]

What symptoms are commonly linked to perimenopause?

Longitudinal research on the transition most often reports hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, vaginal dryness, irregular periods, and cognitive complaints sometimes called brain fog, with psychogenic symptoms including trouble concentrating reported in up to 70% of women during perimenopause and menopause.[1]
