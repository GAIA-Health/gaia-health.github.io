Mood Swings

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Mood swings are noticeable shifts in emotional state over hours or days, logged in health tracking as a subjective symptom. Surveys rank mood symptoms among the most common premenstrual complaints,[1] yet prospective daily-record studies find day-to-day mood tracks the cycle far less tightly than memory suggests.[3]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Mood Swings Measure

A mood-swings log captures how much your emotional state moved around on a given day, not why it moved. It's a subjective symptom, so there's no device or lab draw behind it. Clinical references list mood swings, irritability, anxiety, and anger among the common premenstrual symptoms, alongside physical ones like bloating and breast tenderness.[1]

How It's Measured

By self-report, logged day by day. Researchers studying cycle and mood lean on prospective daily records rather than memory, because asking people to recall past cycles tends to produce different answers than recording each day as it happens.[3] A personal log works the same way: note today's moods today, then let the dates line up against the cycle later.

Why People Track Mood Swings

Mostly to separate the pattern they remember from the pattern that's actually there. Premenstrual symptoms are very common at the population level. In a Swiss survey of 3,913 women, 91% reported at least one.[2] But whether an individual's mood really moves with her cycle is exactly what a daily log can show, and prospective research says the answer varies a lot from person to person.[3]

When mood symptoms cluster in the days before a period, clinical references describe that pattern under PMS, with PMDD as its most severe form.[4] How researchers tell the two apart is covered in our guide PMS vs PMDD.

What the Research Shows

Premenstrual symptoms are common but meeting formal criteria is not. In the Swiss population-based survey, 91% of women reported at least one premenstrual symptom, while 10.3% met criteria for PMS and 3.1% for PMDD.[2]

The link between cycle phase and daily mood is weaker than its reputation. A review of 47 prospective studies found only about 15% showed negative mood tied to the premenstrual phase alone, and the authors cautioned against assuming mood follows the cycle for everyone.[3]

Estimates of how many women experience PMS vary widely by method. One pooled figure across studies puts worldwide PMS prevalence near 48%, far above the stricter survey-based estimates, which shows how much the definition drives the number.[1]

Related Terms

Luteal Phase

The post-ovulation phase where premenstrual symptoms, when they occur, tend to fall.

Progesterone

The hormone that dominates the luteal phase and drops before menstruation.

Estradiol

The main estrogen, which rises and falls twice across a typical cycle.

Nausea

Another commonly logged subjective symptom, tracked across cycle and pregnancy contexts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Are mood changes before a period common?

Yes. In a Swiss population-based survey of 3,913 women, 91% reported at least one premenstrual symptom,[2] and mood-related complaints like irritability and mood swings are among the symptoms clinical references list for PMS.[1]

Does research show mood follows the menstrual cycle?

The evidence is mixed. A review of 47 prospective studies found that only about 15% linked negative mood to the premenstrual phase alone,[3] which is why daily logging matters more than remembering backwards.

What's the difference between PMS and PMDD?

Clinical references describe PMS as recurring premenstrual symptoms that affect daily life, and PMDD as its most severe form.[4] In one population-based survey, 10.3% of women met PMS criteria and 3.1% met PMDD criteria.[2]
