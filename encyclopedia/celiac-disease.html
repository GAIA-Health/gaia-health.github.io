Celiac Disease

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Celiac disease is a chronic autoimmune condition in which eating gluten triggers an immune response that damages the lining of the small intestine, causing villous atrophy.[1] It affects roughly 1% of the US population[1] and is linked to malabsorption of nutrients including iron, folate, and vitamin B12.[1]

What Celiac Disease Is

StatPearls describes celiac disease as an autoimmune inflammatory condition triggered by an immune-mediated response to dietary gluten.[1] That response damages the small intestinal mucosa, leading to villous atrophy, the flattening of the finger-like villi that normally absorb nutrients, along with gastrointestinal symptoms and malabsorption.[1] Genetic predisposition centers on specific immune genes, HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8, though carrying them doesn't mean the disease will develop.[1]

Because the small intestine is where most nutrient absorption happens, celiac disease's downstream effects reach beyond digestion. Fatigue, pallor, and shortness of breath can point to anemia caused by poor absorption of iron, folate, and vitamin B12.[1] Management is a strict, complete gluten-free diet, described in clinical guidelines as the only effective treatment and typically overseen by a clinician and dietitian.[1][2]

Why People Track It

The celiac-anemia connection is well documented: a systematic review and meta-analysis found celiac disease meaningfully more common among people being evaluated for iron-deficiency anemia than in the general population.[3] The mechanism traces back to where the damage happens, iron absorption occurs mainly in the proximal duodenum, the same section of small intestine celiac disease affects first.[4] That's why people managing celiac disease commonly track iron-deficiency anemia and ferritin alongside their digestive symptoms, rather than treating gut and blood-count results as separate stories.

Day to day, the most frequently logged symptoms are digestive: bloating, stomach pain, and other digestive changes, alongside fatigue from the malabsorption itself.[1]

What the Research Shows

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis in Gastroenterology found celiac disease prevalence was meaningfully elevated among patients being worked up for iron-deficiency anemia compared with the general population, supporting anemia screening as part of the celiac evaluation picture.[3]

A 2021 review in Nutrients traces the mechanism directly to intestinal anatomy: because iron absorption is concentrated in the proximal duodenum, and that's the segment celiac disease damages earliest and most severely, impaired absorption there is a primary driver of celiac-related anemia.[4]

The American College of Gastroenterology's 2023 guideline update on celiac disease diagnosis and management reaffirms villous atrophy on biopsy, alongside serologic antibody testing, as central to how the condition is confirmed.[2]

Related Terms

Iron-Deficiency Anemia

A well-documented downstream effect of celiac-related malabsorption.

The lab marker of iron stores most often tracked alongside a celiac evaluation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What is celiac disease?

Celiac disease is a chronic autoimmune condition in which eating gluten triggers an immune response that damages the lining of the small intestine, causing villous atrophy. It affects roughly 1% of the US population.[1]

Why is celiac disease linked to iron-deficiency anemia?

Iron is absorbed mainly in the proximal duodenum, the same section of small intestine celiac disease damages first. A systematic review found celiac disease meaningfully more common among people being evaluated for iron-deficiency anemia than in the general population.[3][4]

Is celiac disease the same as gluten sensitivity?

No. Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition confirmed by specific antibody testing and intestinal biopsy findings. Non-celiac gluten sensitivity can produce similar symptoms without that same autoimmune or intestinal-damage profile.[1]
