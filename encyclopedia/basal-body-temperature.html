Basal Body Temperature (BBT)

Cycle & Fertility · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Basal body temperature is the lowest resting temperature the body reaches, measured immediately upon waking before any movement, eating, or drinking.[1] It rises slightly after ovulation because progesterone raises the hypothalamus's temperature set point, so a temperature chart confirms ovulation after the fact rather than predicting it in advance.[3]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What BBT Measures

BBT reflects the body's baseline temperature at complete rest, before any activity raises it. Across the menstrual cycle it follows a two-phase pattern: lower in the follicular phase (before ovulation) and higher through the luteal phase (after ovulation), because the corpus luteum that forms after an egg is released produces progesterone, and progesterone acts on the hypothalamus to raise the body's temperature set point.[1] Reviews of temperature regulation across the cycle put the post-ovulatory rise at roughly 0.3–0.7°C (about 0.5–1°F) above the follicular baseline.[2]

How It's Measured

Oral BBT is a single daily reading, taken by mouth with a thermometer sensitive to a tenth of a degree, at the same time each morning before getting up, talking, or drinking anything. Any of those can shift the reading enough to obscure the signal.[1] Wearables measure something related but distinct: wrist skin temperature, sampled continuously overnight rather than as one point-in-time reading. A prospective comparison of the two methods found wrist skin temperature picked up more true post-ovulatory shifts than oral BBT (sensitivity 0.62 vs. 0.23) but also flagged more false shifts, so the two aren't interchangeable readings of the same thing.[4]

Why People Track BBT

A sustained temperature rise is one of the few signals that a cycle was ovulatory: an egg was actually released, not just that a period arrived. Because the shift shows up only after ovulation, people typically chart it alongside earlier fertile-window signals like cervical mucus, using BBT as the after-the-fact confirmation rather than the forecast.

What the Research Shows

The post-ovulatory shift itself is well documented, but the exact pathway connecting progesterone to the hypothalamus's temperature set point is still being worked out.[2]

A single oral reading also moves with sleep, stress, and circadian rhythm, so researchers who compared the methods describe oral BBT as a noisier signal for catching the shift than continuous overnight sampling.[4]

Both methods share the same fundamental limit: temperature only moves after ovulation has already happened, which is why the same research team that validated wrist tracking against oral BBT calls temperature-based methods "a retrospective indication of the fertile window."[3]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Can basal body temperature predict ovulation before it happens?

No. BBT only rises after ovulation has already occurred, so a chart confirms ovulation retrospectively rather than forecasting it. Researchers describe temperature-based tracking as a retrospective indicator of the fertile window for this reason.[3]

Is wrist temperature from a wearable the same as basal body temperature?

No. Oral BBT is a single reading taken by mouth at wake-up, while wrist devices record skin temperature continuously overnight. They track the same underlying hormonal shift but are different measurements with different sensitivity and noise profiles.[4]

Why does temperature rise after ovulation?

The corpus luteum, formed from the follicle after an egg is released, produces progesterone. Progesterone acts on the hypothalamus and raises the body's temperature set point, producing a small, sustained rise for the rest of the luteal phase.[1]
