Period Flow

Cycle & Fertility · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Period flow is the amount of menstrual bleeding during a period, usually logged day by day on a light, medium, or heavy scale. Research measures it directly with the alkaline hematin lab method or estimates it with pictorial charts that count pads and tampons.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Period Flow Measures

Period flow describes bleeding volume, which is a separate question from how long a period lasts or how often one arrives. Two people with the same cycle length can have very different flow, and one person's flow usually changes across the days of a single period. In a study of 201 regularly menstruating women, bleeding was heaviest during the first three days of menstruation.[4]

How It's Measured

The research gold standard is the alkaline hematin method, a lab technique that extracts hemoglobin from collected pads and tampons and converts it to an actual blood volume.[1] Nobody does that at home, so studies also use pictorial charts like the PBAC, which score the number of pads or tampons used and how soaked each one is.[2] Everyday tracking works the same way but simpler: you rate each day as light, medium, or heavy, and product counts or a menstrual cup's markings serve as rough proxies for volume.

Why People Track Period Flow

A single heavy day tells you little, but months of flow logs show your own baseline and how it shifts over time. Flow also connects to iron. Menstruation is a recurring, measurable form of blood loss, and in a 2024 study of 473 premenopausal blood donors it independently predicted both hemoglobin and ferritin levels.[5] That makes flow logs and iron-panel results two sides of the same balance, worth seeing on one timeline.

What the Research Shows

In a real-world dataset of 612,613 cycles from app users, the mean period length was 4.0 days with a standard deviation of 1.5 days.[3]

A systematic review of measurement methods treats the alkaline hematin technique as the gold standard for quantifying menstrual blood loss, and concludes that pictorial methods strike a good balance between ease of use and validated accuracy.[1] The original PBAC validation study showed that scores built from product counts and soiling correlate with directly measured blood loss.[2]

On the iron side, menstrual blood loss was an independent determinant of hemoglobin and ferritin levels in a cohort of 473 premenopausal blood donors, alongside factors like time since a person's last donation.[5]

Related Terms

Cycle Length

The day count each period starts, with day 1 defined by the first day of flow.

Spotting & Intermenstrual Bleeding

Light bleeding outside the period window, logged separately from flow.

Ferritin

The iron-storage marker that population studies tie to menstrual blood loss.

Hemoglobin

The oxygen-carrying protein measured alongside ferritin in iron research.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

How do researchers actually measure menstrual blood loss?

The gold standard is the alkaline hematin method, a lab technique that extracts hemoglobin from collected pads and tampons and converts it to a blood volume.[1] Because that's impractical outside a study, researchers also use pictorial charts like the PBAC, which score the number of products used and how soaked each one is.[2]

Are pad and tampon counts an accurate way to gauge flow?

They're a proxy, not a measurement. Validated pictorial charts score both the number of products used and how soiled each one is, and those scores correlate with directly measured blood loss.[2]

Why log period flow next to lab results like ferritin?

Menstrual blood loss is a recurring form of blood loss, and a 2024 study of 473 premenopausal blood donors found it independently predicted both hemoglobin and ferritin levels.[5] Flow logs and iron-panel results describe two sides of the same balance, so it helps to see them on one timeline.
