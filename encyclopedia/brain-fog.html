Brain Fog

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Brain fog is a lay term for fuzzy thinking, word-finding trouble, and forgetfulness that some women notice during parts of the menstrual cycle and, more consistently, across the menopause transition.[3] It isn't a formal diagnosis. It's a cluster of subjective cognitive complaints most often involving verbal memory, verbal fluency, and processing speed.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Brain Fog Is

"Brain fog" isn't a medical term, it's the phrase people reach for when thinking feels slower or harder to hold onto than usual. In the research literature it shows up as subjective cognitive complaints: trouble finding a word mid-sentence, losing the thread of a conversation, walking into a room and forgetting why, or needing more effort to concentrate on work that used to feel automatic. A systematic review and meta-analysis of cognition across the menopause transition found that verbal memory and verbal fluency, the ability to retrieve words quickly, differed by menopause stage, with postmenopausal women showing the clearest deficits, alongside a higher rate of depressive symptoms in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women.[2] A short clinical review adds that these complaints are common during perimenopause and tend to track alongside other perimenopausal changes, including shifting estrogen levels, vasomotor symptoms, sleep disruption, and mood, rather than estrogen decline acting alone. It also notes that only about 11% to 13% of women show clinically significant impairment on formal testing, meaning most brain fog stays well short of that.[3]

The picture is different for the regular menstrual cycle outside of perimenopause. A review of menstrual-cycle research found that cycle-linked changes in emotion and mood are fairly consistent and reproducible across studies, especially tied to the luteal-phase rise in progesterone, but that methodologically sound studies have not found reliable cycle-phase differences in cognitive performance itself.[4] That's part of why brain fog is discussed far more as a perimenopause and menopause-transition symptom than as a routine monthly one.

How It's Logged

There's no lab test or wearable reading for brain fog. It's a logged symptom, recorded the same way as a headache or a mood dip: you note that a day felt foggy and roughly how much it interfered, which turns a hard-to-pin-down feeling into a data point you can look back on across weeks and cycles.

Why People Track Brain Fog

Brain fog is easy to misread as a one-off bad day when you're living it and easy to underestimate in memory once it's passed. A dated log turns a vague sense of "my head's been foggy lately" into a pattern you can actually look at: which days it shows up, whether it clusters in a particular part of your cycle or stretch of perimenopause, and how it lines up with sleep, mood, or hot flashes logged the same day.

What the Research Shows

The Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) followed 2,362 women over four years and found that perimenopausal women, unlike premenopausal or postmenopausal women, did not improve on repeated processing-speed and delayed verbal-memory tests, a pattern the researchers interpreted as a temporary dip in cognitive performance tied to the perimenopausal years rather than a lasting one.[1]

A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies spanning premenopause through postmenopause found cognitive differences by stage, particularly in verbal memory and verbal fluency, with postmenopausal women performing worst on both, alongside an increased likelihood of depressive symptoms in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.[2]

Outside the menopause transition, a review of menstrual-cycle studies found emotional and mood-related changes across the cycle were consistently reproducible, but cognitive-performance differences by cycle phase were not, which is why the strongest brain-fog evidence sits in perimenopause rather than the regular cycle.[4]

Related Terms

Hot Flashes

Another perimenopausal symptom often logged on the same foggy, disrupted days.

Fatigue

Low energy that frequently overlaps with foggy thinking on the same days.

Insomnia

Disrupted sleep is one of the factors research ties to next-day cognitive complaints.

The main estrogen whose shifts across perimenopause are studied alongside cognitive complaints.

Mood changes that research links more consistently to cycle and hormone shifts than cognition is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Does brain fog during perimenopause last?

In the research, it's usually described as a temporary pattern. A longitudinal SWAN analysis found the drop in processing speed and verbal memory was limited to the perimenopausal years and resolved after the transition.[1] Most women's testing stays in the normal range, though a smaller share, around 11 to 13% in one review, show clinically significant impairment.[3]

Does brain fog happen during the regular menstrual cycle, or only around menopause?

The evidence is strongest for the menopause transition. A review of menstrual-cycle research found emotional changes, like mood and reactivity, are consistently linked to cycle phase, but well-designed studies haven't found reliable cognitive-performance differences across a regular cycle.[4] Brain fog as a cognitive complaint is documented far more consistently in perimenopause.

Which cognitive skills does brain fog affect most?

Studies point most often to verbal memory, verbal fluency, working memory, and processing speed. A SWAN analysis found perimenopausal women didn't improve on repeated processing-speed and verbal-memory tests the way pre- and postmenopausal women did,[1] and a separate meta-analysis found verbal memory and verbal fluency differed by menopause stage, with postmenopausal women showing the largest differences.[2]
