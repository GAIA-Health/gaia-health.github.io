Magnesium (Serum)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Magnesium is the second most abundant mineral inside human cells, acting as a cofactor in more than 300 enzymatic reactions.[1] About 99% of it sits in bone and soft tissue rather than blood, so a serum result reflects only a small circulating fraction.[2] It's measured as serum magnesium from a standard blood draw.

What Magnesium Measures

Magnesium is the fourth most common cation in the body overall and the second most common one inside cells, after potassium.[1] It's a cofactor for more than 300 enzymatic reactions, including ones involved in ATP production, muscle contraction and relaxation, nerve signaling, and cardiac conduction.[1]

Almost all of it, though, isn't where a blood draw can see it. Roughly 99% of the body's magnesium is stored in bone and soft tissue, with only a sliver circulating in serum at any given time.[2] There's also no clinically available test for ionized, biologically active magnesium, so labs measure the total amount instead, a limitation that means a serum result can look unremarkable even when the body's broader magnesium status has shifted.[2]

How It's Measured

Magnesium is measured from a standard blood draw and reported as serum magnesium. Labs commonly order it alongside other electrolytes like calcium and potassium, since the three are managed by overlapping physiological systems and a change in one can affect how the others get read.[2] Each lab sets and reports its own reference interval with the result.

Why People Track Magnesium

Magnesium comes almost entirely from what you eat. Green leafy vegetables, fish, legumes, and whole grains are among the foods richest in it.[1] It's also one of the most talked-about minerals in wellness content, credited with everything from better sleep to fewer cramps. The guideline-grade research is narrower than that conversation suggests.

A 2025 systematic review of randomized controlled trials looked at nutritional interventions for the psychological symptoms of premenstrual syndrome. It found consistent, positive effects for vitamin B6, calcium, and zinc across multiple trials, but concluded there was insufficient evidence to support an effect from magnesium supplementation, drawing on three small trials with inconsistent individual results rather than a broader, consistent evidence base.[3] Individual studies on magnesium and menstrual symptoms have reported benefits, but the trials are few, small, and inconsistent in dose and design, and they haven't added up to the kind of evidence guidelines are built on. What the Research Shows

Magnesium's role as a cofactor spans more than 300 enzymatic reactions, touching processes from ATP production to cardiac conduction.[1]

Serum magnesium has a real measurement limitation. There's no clinically available test for the ionized, active form, and roughly 99% of body magnesium sits outside the bloodstream in bone and soft tissue, so a single serum reading is an imperfect window into overall status.[2]

On premenstrual symptoms specifically, a 2025 systematic review of randomized controlled trials found insufficient evidence to support an effect from magnesium supplementation on the psychological symptoms of PMS, in contrast to consistent positive effects found for vitamin B6, calcium, and zinc.[3] That conclusion rested on three small trials whose individual results were inconsistent with each other, not a single trial or a larger consistent body of evidence.[3]

Does magnesium supplementation help with PMS?

The evidence is mixed. A 2025 systematic review of randomized controlled trials found insufficient evidence to support an effect of magnesium supplementation on the psychological symptoms of PMS, based on the small number of trials available and their inconsistent results, while nutrients like vitamin B6, calcium, and zinc showed more consistent positive effects across multiple studies. Why is a single serum magnesium result hard to interpret on its own?

About 99% of the body's magnesium is stored in bone and soft tissue, not blood, and there's no clinically available test for the active, ionized form. That means a serum result reflects only a small circulating fraction, and it doesn't necessarily move in step with the body's broader magnesium status.
