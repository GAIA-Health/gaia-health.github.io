Tingling & Numbness (Paresthesia)

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Paresthesia is the medical term for tingling, prickling, or numb sensations on the skin that happen without an outside cause like pressure, cold, or sitting on a limb wrong.[1] It's a symptom of sensory nerve signals that are firing abnormally or getting interrupted on their way to the brain.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Tingling & Numbness Measure

Sensory nerves carry information from receptors in the skin, like mechanoreceptors for touch and pressure and thermoreceptors for temperature, to the brain.[1] Larger, myelinated fibers carry that signal fast, while smaller unmyelinated fibers carry it slower, which is part of why a single stimulus can register in stages instead of all at once.[1] Tingling reflects nerve fibers firing when they shouldn't, and numbness reflects the opposite: a signal that isn't getting through at all, whether from pressure on a nerve or damage further along the pathway.[1]

How It's Logged

There's no wearable sensor or lab value for paresthesia. It's logged as a self-reported symptom: where it happened, how it felt, and when, turning an odd sensation that's easy to shrug off in the moment into a dated entry you can look back on.

Why People Track Tingling & Numbness

Paresthesia shows up in two very different contexts that people often want to keep separate in a log. One is mechanical: nerve compression, most often at the wrist, and pregnancy is a well-documented risk factor for it because of fluid retention and hormonal changes.[3] The other is metabolic: nutrient status, particularly vitamin B12, which the nerves that carry sensory signals depend on to stay properly insulated.[2]

Logging where and when tingling or numbness happens, alongside what else you're tracking that day, turns a vague and easy-to-forget sensation into a pattern with actual shape.

What the Research Shows

Vitamin B12 is a cofactor the body needs to convert methylmalonic acid into a usable form. When B12 is deficient, methylmalonic acid and homocysteine build up, and both are linked to damage to myelin, the insulating sheath around nerve fibers, which is the proposed mechanism behind the paresthesia, numbness, and peripheral neuropathy seen in B12 deficiency.[2] Left untreated, the same process is linked to degeneration in the spinal cord pathways that carry proprioception and touch.[2]

Carpal tunnel syndrome, compression of the median nerve at the wrist, is described as the most common entrapment neuropathy, causing pain, numbness, and tingling in the thumb and first three fingers, and it affects women roughly three times as often as men.[4] In a cross-sectional study of 482 women in their third trimester of pregnancy, 111 had signs and symptoms consistent with carpal tunnel syndrome, a prevalence of 23.03%, with left-handedness, gestational diabetes, and maternal age all associated with it.[3] Research on pregnancy-related carpal tunnel syndrome also describes symptoms as generally resolving after birth.[4]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What causes tingling and numbness (paresthesia)?

Paresthesia happens when sensory nerves fire abnormally, producing tingling, or when their signal to the brain gets interrupted, producing numbness.[1] It's studied in connection with nerve compression, like carpal tunnel syndrome, and with metabolic causes, like vitamin B12 deficiency.[2][4]

Is tingling and numbness linked to vitamin B12?

Yes. Vitamin B12 deficiency is well studied as a cause of neurologic symptoms including paresthesia, numbness, and tingling. Low B12 lets methylmalonic acid and homocysteine build up, and both are linked to myelin damage along the nerves that carry sensory signals.[2]

Why do pregnant women get tingling and numbness in their hands?

Carpal tunnel syndrome, compression of the median nerve at the wrist, is common in pregnancy. A study of 482 women in their third trimester found signs and symptoms consistent with carpal tunnel syndrome in just over 23% of them.[3]
