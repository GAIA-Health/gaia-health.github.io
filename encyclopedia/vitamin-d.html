Vitamin D (25-Hydroxyvitamin D)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

The 25-hydroxyvitamin D test, written 25(OH)D, is the standard blood test for vitamin D status.[1] It measures the stable circulating form the liver makes from vitamin D, whether that vitamin D came from sunlight on skin or from food, and it's run on a standard blood draw.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What the 25-OH Test Measures

A "vitamin D test" almost always measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D. Your liver makes this form from the vitamin D you take in, and your kidneys later convert a small amount of it into 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, the active hormone.[2] Labs measure the 25-OH form instead of the active form because it sticks around. 25(OH)D has a half-life of about two weeks in blood, while the active form clears in under four hours.[1] That long half-life makes 25(OH)D a steady read on the vitamin D your body has accumulated over recent weeks, not a snapshot of one hormonal moment.

How It's Measured

The test runs on a standard venous blood draw, and results come back in nanograms per milliliter or nanomoles per liter depending on the lab. One honest caveat: different assay methods can return different numbers for the same blood sample, which is why an international standardization program exists for this test and why researchers still debate how to compare results across labs.[4] Your lab reports its own reference interval with each result.

Why People Track Vitamin D

Vitamin D's core job is calcium handling. It stimulates calcium and phosphorus absorption in the intestine and helps keep blood calcium in the range bone mineralization depends on.[1] Your body gets it from two directions: skin makes vitamin D3 when UVB sunlight hits it, and food supplies the rest, mainly oily fish like salmon and sardines plus fortified staples like milk and cereal.[1],[2]

Because sunlight drives so much of the supply, this is one of the few lab values with a visible calendar rhythm. A study of nearly 3.5 million US serum samples found population 25(OH)D levels peak in late August and bottom out in February, trailing the solstices by about eight weeks.[5] That seasonality is exactly what makes it more interesting to chart over time than to read as a single number.

What the Research Shows

The case for testing the 25-OH form rests on its stability: a roughly two-week half-life in blood, against under four hours for the active 1,25-dihydroxy form.[1]

Seasonality is real and large-scale. Across nearly 3.5 million US samples, 25(OH)D followed a lagged seasonal wave, peaking in late summer and troughing in late winter.[5]

What counts as a "sufficient" level is genuinely unsettled. The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline found no clear evidence defining an optimal 25(OH)D target for disease prevention and suggested against routine testing in the healthy populations it reviewed.[3] Researchers have argued that inconsistent assay standardization is part of why guidelines keep disagreeing.[4] That's why this entry describes the test and skips the thresholds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Why do labs test 25-hydroxyvitamin D instead of active vitamin D?

Because it lasts. 25-hydroxyvitamin D has a half-life of about two weeks in blood, while the active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, clears in under four hours. The 25-OH form gives a steadier picture of the vitamin D your body has taken in over recent weeks, so it's the standard status marker.

Does Go Go Gaia test vitamin D?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't draw blood or run labs. You get a 25-hydroxyvitamin D result from a blood draw ordered through a clinician or lab service, then upload it to your Go Go Gaia account, where it's stored and charted alongside your other tracked data.

Is there an agreed optimal vitamin D level?

Not really. The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline found no clear evidence defining an optimal target level for disease prevention, and different assay methods can return different numbers on the same sample. Labs report their own reference interval with each result, and questions about your own result belong with your doctor.
