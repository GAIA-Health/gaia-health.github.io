Night Sweats

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Night sweats are episodes of heavy sweating during sleep, often enough to dampen sleepwear or bedding. They're the nighttime form of vasomotor symptoms, sharing physiology with hot flashes, and are commonly reported during the menopause transition.[1] Studies also find them across many other populations and life stages.[3]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Night Sweats Are

Researchers group night sweats with hot flashes as vasomotor symptoms: episodes of intense internal heat with profuse sweating and widened blood vessels near the skin.[2] The physiology is the same whether the episode hits at 2 pm or 2 am. In women with frequent symptoms, the thermoneutral zone, the range of core temperature the body tolerates before sweating or shivering, narrows to nearly zero, so small rises in core temperature set off the full response.[2] Night sweats also show up well beyond the menopause transition. A systematic review found prevalence estimates from 10% of older primary care patients to 60% in some clinical populations, and described the symptom as nonspecific.[3]

How It's Logged

Night sweats are a logged symptom rather than a device reading. You record on waking that an episode happened, which builds a dated record you can set against sleep data, cycle phase, and skin-temperature trends from a wearable.

Why People Track Night Sweats

A single sweaty night tells you little. A dated log across months shows whether episodes cluster in certain cycle phases, follow certain days, or track with sleep quality. Because night sweats happen while you're asleep, the morning log is also the only reliable record you get. Pairing it with wearable sleep and temperature data fills in what you slept through.

What the Research Shows

SWAN analyses report that 60% to 80% of women experience vasomotor symptoms at some point during the menopause transition, with occurrence peaking in late perimenopause and the early postmenopausal years.[4] In the SWAN duration study, the median total course of frequent vasomotor symptoms was 7.4 years.[5]

Sleep research links the two directly. Self-reported vasomotor symptoms are consistently associated with poorer self-reported sleep quality, and in one objective analysis an awakening coincided with about 69% of measured hot flash events. Wake time attributed to those events accounted for roughly 27% of time awake after sleep onset on average.[1]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Are night sweats the same as hot flashes?

They share the same underlying physiology. Researchers group hot flashes and night sweats together as vasomotor symptoms, episodes of intense heat with sweating and flushing. Night sweats are simply the episodes that happen during sleep.[2]

How common are night sweats?

A systematic review found prevalence estimates ranging from 10% among older primary care patients to 60% in some clinical populations, and concluded the symptom is nonspecific.[3] During the menopause transition, SWAN analyses report that 60% to 80% of women experience vasomotor symptoms at some point.[4]

Do night sweats affect sleep?

Research consistently links self-reported vasomotor symptoms with poorer self-reported sleep quality. In one objective analysis, an awakening coincided with about 69% of measured hot flash events, and wake time attributed to them accounted for roughly 27% of time awake after sleep onset on average.[1]
