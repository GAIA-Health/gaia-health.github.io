Vitamin B6

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Vitamin B6 exists in three natural forms, pyridoxine, pyridoxal, and pyridoxamine, all of which the body converts into the active coenzyme pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP).[1] PLP is a cofactor in more than 100 enzymatic reactions, including amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis.[1][2] The body can't store it, so it needs regular dietary intake.[2] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks it from the meals you log.

What Vitamin B6 Does

Vitamin B6 exists in three natural forms, pyridoxine, pyridoxal, and pyridoxamine, and all three convert into the same active coenzyme, pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP or P5P), inside the body.[1] PLP is the form that does the work: it functions as a coenzyme in amino acid, protein, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism, and in neurotransmitter synthesis.[1] Altogether, it's a cofactor in more than 100 enzymatic reactions, catalyzing processes including transamination, decarboxylation, racemization, and elimination.[1]

That range of reactions reaches beyond metabolism narrowly defined. Vitamin B6 also contributes to neurotransmitter synthesis, interleukin-2 production, and hemoglobin formation, and it's involved in the first steps of porphyrin synthesis through its role in transamination and decarboxylation.[2] Like other B vitamins, it's water-soluble, and the body can't store it, so a daily supply is needed to keep up with demand.[2]

Why People Track Vitamin B6

Vitamin B6 comes up often in conversations about premenstrual syndrome, and the research base behind it is comparatively solid next to some of the other nutrients in that conversation. A 2025 systematic review of randomized controlled trials looked at nutritional interventions for the psychological symptoms of PMS and found that treatment with vitamin B6, calcium, and zinc consistently had significant positive effects on those symptoms across the trials it reviewed.[3] That's a notably more consistent result than the same review reached for magnesium, where the evidence was judged insufficient to support an effect. What the Research Shows

Vitamin B6's mechanism is well characterized: as PLP, it's a cofactor in more than 100 enzymatic reactions spanning amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism, along with neurotransmitter synthesis, interleukin-2 production, and hemoglobin formation.[1][2]

On premenstrual symptoms specifically, a 2025 systematic review of randomized controlled trials drew on multiple trials of vitamin B6 for the psychological symptoms of PMS and concluded that vitamin B6, alongside calcium and zinc, showed a consistent positive effect across the trials reviewed, a stronger and more consistent finding than the same review reached for magnesium.[3]
