White Blood Cell (WBC) Count

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

A white blood cell (WBC) count measures the number of white blood cells, the cells of the immune system, in a set volume of blood.[1] It's reported as part of a complete blood count (CBC), and a differential breaks the total into five cell types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils.[2]

What a WBC Count Measures

White blood cells, also called leukocytes, are part of the immune system. They circulate in the blood and mount responses to injury and pathogens.[1] A WBC count reports how many of these cells circulate in each unit of blood at the moment of the draw. The count covers five cell types. Neutrophils act as the body's first responders to bacteria. Lymphocytes make up the adaptive immune system, the part that learns and remembers. Monocytes can leave the bloodstream and mature into macrophages in tissue. Eosinophils respond to parasites, and basophils take part in allergic responses.[1]

How It's Measured

A WBC count comes from a standard venous blood draw, counted by an automated analyzer as part of a CBC. The same panel reports red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelets, and most analyzers now generate a leukocyte differential automatically, so the total count and its five-way breakdown usually arrive on the same report.[2] Labs report their own reference interval with each result, and that interval can vary by lab and by method.

Why People Track a WBC Count

The WBC count sits on nearly every routine blood panel, so anyone who gets periodic lab work ends up with a series of these numbers over time. The count shifts with normal life states, and some of those shifts follow the menstrual cycle. In a 1975 study that sampled women through their cycles, neutrophil counts varied cyclically, with the most consistent pattern being a fall in neutrophils at menstruation.[3] A pattern like that only shows up when results are dated and lined up against cycle days.

What the Research Shows

Exercise raises the WBC count right away, and the size of that rise tracks the intensity and duration of the work. It's a normal response, driven in part by stress hormones like catecholamines and cortisol.[4]

Pregnancy shifts the count too. White blood cell counts run higher in normal pregnancy than in the non-pregnant state, a documented physiologic change of pregnancy itself.[5]

The menstrual cycle shows up too. Neutrophil counts varied cyclically in women sampled across their cycles, falling most consistently at menstruation, with hormone changes preceding the count changes by a day or two.[3]

What is a WBC differential?

A differential splits the total white blood cell count into its five cell types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils. Most modern lab analyzers generate a differential automatically alongside the total count, so both usually appear on the same CBC report.

Is a WBC count the same thing as a CBC?

No. The WBC count is one line on a complete blood count (CBC), which also reports red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelets, and red cell indices. The CBC is the panel, and the WBC count is one of the values inside it.
