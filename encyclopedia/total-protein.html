Total Protein

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Total protein is a blood test that adds up every protein circulating in plasma, mainly albumin and a family of proteins called globulins.[2] Labs report the combined value alongside albumin so the albumin-to-globulin (A/G) ratio can be calculated, from the same standard blood draw used for a liver panel.[2]

What Total Protein Measures

Blood plasma carries more than one kind of protein, and total protein is the combined weight of all of them in a single number. Roughly half of that total is albumin, the liver-made protein that holds fluid inside blood vessels and transports substances through the body.[1] Most of the remainder is globulin, which isn't one protein but a family of them, split by electrophoretic mobility into alpha, beta, and gamma fractions that each carry different jobs.[4]

The two families come from different places. The liver makes albumin and most of the alpha and beta globulins, including proteins involved in transport and blood clotting. The gamma globulin fraction is mostly immunoglobulins, the antibodies plasma cells make with support from lymphoid tissue like the spleen and lymph nodes, rather than the liver.[4] That split is what the A/G ratio is built to describe: how much of a person's total protein came from the liver's manufacturing versus the immune system's.

How It's Measured

Total protein is measured from a standard venous blood draw and reported in grams per deciliter (g/dL). It's a routine component of the liver panel and the comprehensive metabolic panel, usually printed on the same report as albumin, with the A/G ratio calculated from the two.[2],[3] Each lab sets and reports its own reference interval with the result.

Why People Track Total Protein

Total protein comes back on almost any comprehensive metabolic or liver panel, so it's a value most people already have several copies of scattered across old lab reports. Because it's a sum of two protein families made by two different systems, tracking it alongside albumin over time gives more context than either number does alone. A dated series shows whether the two are moving together or diverging, which is a different question than what any single draw can answer on its own.

What the Research Shows

Physiology references describe blood plasma protein as split mainly between albumin and a globulin fraction that itself divides into alpha, beta, and gamma sub-fractions, each with distinct functions from transport to clotting to immunity.[4]

The liver is responsible for producing albumin and most non-immune plasma proteins, while immunoglobulins, the gamma globulin component, come from plasma cells rather than hepatic synthesis, which is the physiological basis for reading total protein alongside the A/G ratio rather than as one undifferentiated number.[4]

Total protein and albumin are grouped together in standard liver-panel and comprehensive-metabolic-panel test menus specifically because the two values are read as a pair.[3]

Related Terms

Albumin

The single largest protein counted inside the total protein value.

ALT

A liver enzyme reported on the same panel as total protein.

AST

Another routine liver-panel enzyme, distinct from the protein values.

Bilirubin

A pigment measured on the same liver panel as total protein and albumin.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the albumin-to-globulin (A/G) ratio?

It's a comparison of the two main protein groups that make up total protein: albumin, made almost entirely by the liver, and globulins, made partly by the liver and partly by the immune system. Labs calculate the ratio from the same blood draw that gives total protein and albumin.

What's the difference between total protein and albumin?

Albumin is one specific protein and the single largest contributor to total protein. Total protein is the sum of albumin plus globulins, a broader group of proteins that includes antibodies. The two are reported as separate values on the same panel.
