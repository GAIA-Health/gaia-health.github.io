Caffeine

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Caffeine is a naturally occurring central nervous system stimulant in the methylxanthine class, found in coffee, tea, and cacao, and added to sodas and energy drinks.[1] It works mainly by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain.[1] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks it in milligrams from the meals and drinks you log.

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Caffeine Does

Caffeine is the most widely used psychoactive stimulant in the world, sourced mainly from coffee beans but also occurring naturally in tea, cacao beans, kola nuts, yerba mate, and the guarana berry, and added to many sodas and energy drinks.[1] It acts by antagonizing all four adenosine receptor subtypes, and the antagonism of the A2a receptor specifically is what's responsible for the wakefulness effect people feel from it.[1]

How long caffeine sticks around varies a lot by who's taking it. In an average adult, the half-life is roughly 5 hours, but it's notably longer in some groups and shorter in others. Smokers clear caffeine up to 50% faster than nonsmokers, while pregnant people, especially in the third trimester, can have a half-life prolonged to as long as 15 hours.[1]

Why People Track Caffeine

Caffeine's slow, individual clearance is exactly why people track the timing of it, not just the total. Go Go Gaia's free caffeine calculator works backward from your bedtime, drink, and metabolism speed to estimate a personal cutoff time.

Hormones change that math. Chronic use of low-dose estrogen-containing oral contraceptives has been shown to meaningfully slow caffeine clearance: one pharmacokinetic study found an average half-life of 7.9 hours in oral contraceptive users versus 5.4 hours in matched non-users.[4] The natural menstrual cycle is a murkier picture. A study that measured CYP1A2-dependent caffeine metabolism directly across the cycle found no significant difference in clearance between the follicular and luteal phases, concluding that dose adjustment by cycle phase wasn't warranted based on that pharmacokinetic marker.[3] Research on this specific question is genuinely mixed rather than settled.

What the Research Shows

ACOG's Committee Opinion No. 462 describes moderate caffeine consumption, defined there as less than 200 mg per day, as not appearing to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth, based on the evidence available to the committee. The opinion states plainly that caffeine's relationship to fetal growth restriction remains undetermined.[2] That's a description of a professional body's published position, not a recommendation from Go Go Gaia.

The extended half-life seen in pregnancy has a documented mechanism behind it: caffeine crosses the placenta, and pregnancy, especially in the third trimester, is one of the conditions StatPearls lists as prolonging caffeine's half-life well beyond the roughly 5-hour adult average.[1]

On hormonal contraceptives, the pharmacokinetic study found that oral contraceptive users had both a longer half-life (7.9 vs. 5.4 hours) and a markedly slower plasma clearance rate (1.05 vs. 1.75 mL/min/kg) than non-users, pointing to impaired activity of the liver enzyme that breaks caffeine down.[4] That's a different, more consistent finding than the natural-cycle research, where at least one directly measured study found no phase effect at all.[3]

No. Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks caffeine in milligrams from the meals and drinks you log. The Encyclopedia doesn't publish personal intake limits. It describes what professional bodies like ACOG have published as attributed information, not as guidance for any individual reader.

What does ACOG say about caffeine during pregnancy?

ACOG's Committee Opinion No. 462 states that moderate caffeine consumption, which it describes as less than 200 mg per day, doesn't appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth. The opinion notes that caffeine's relationship to fetal growth restriction remains undetermined. This is a description of ACOG's published position, not personalized advice.

Does hormonal birth control or cycle phase change how fast caffeine clears?

Combined hormonal birth control appears to slow caffeine clearance. A classic pharmacokinetic study found caffeine's half-life averaged 7.9 hours in users of low-dose estrogen-containing oral contraceptives versus 5.4 hours in non-users. The natural menstrual cycle is a less settled question: one study measuring CYP1A2 activity directly found no significant difference in caffeine clearance between the follicular and luteal phases.
