Endometriosis

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Endometriosis is a chronic, estrogen-dependent condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside it, most often on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and pelvic lining, triggering inflammation and scar tissue.[2] It's estimated to affect about 10% of women and girls of reproductive age worldwide.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Endometriosis Is

Researchers describe several ways the misplaced tissue is thought to get there. The most studied theory, retrograde menstruation, holds that menstrual tissue flows backward through the fallopian tubes into the pelvis, where cells implant on the surfaces there instead of shedding with a period.[2] Other proposed mechanisms include existing pelvic cells transforming into endometrial-like tissue, cells spreading through blood or lymphatic vessels, and stem cells differentiating into lesions at distant sites.[2] No single theory accounts for every case, and endometriosis is understood today as an inflammatory, estrogen-dependent disease rather than one structural problem.[2]

The hallmark symptoms named in the research are chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea (painful periods), dyspareunia (pain during sex), and infertility.[2] Heavy menstrual bleeding, digestive symptoms like bloating and nausea, urinary symptoms, and fatigue are also commonly reported.[1][2] One consistent finding across studies is that symptom severity doesn't reliably track with how much disease is present on imaging or at surgery, so two people with a similar amount of tissue can report very different symptom burdens.[2]

Laparoscopic surgery, which lets a surgeon see the tissue directly, was long the only way to confirm a diagnosis.[2] Current clinical guidelines lean more on a detailed history, symptom pattern, and pelvic exam, supported by transvaginal ultrasound or MRI, to reach a working diagnosis without requiring surgery first, though a normal scan doesn't rule the condition out.[1][2]

Why People Track the Symptoms of Endometriosis

Time from first symptoms to a confirmed diagnosis is a persistent research focus. A systematic review of 17 studies found that time to diagnosis has ranged from under a year to about 12 years depending on how it's defined and where the study was done, and identified misinterpretation or normalization of symptoms, more often on the physician side than the patient side, as a leading driver of the delay.[3] The World Health Organization cites an average time to diagnosis between 4 and 12 years.[1]

That gap is part of why longitudinal symptom logs come up in endometriosis research and care. Diaries built specifically for the condition and used in clinical studies track cyclic pelvic pain, non-cyclic pelvic pain, and pain during sex as separate dimensions rather than one combined symptom, because each carries different information.[4] A dated record also captures symptoms closer to when they happen instead of relying on memory weeks or months later, which matters when symptom normalization is already part of what slows diagnosis down.[3][4] People living with endometriosis commonly log pelvic pain, cramps, heavy menstrual bleeding, fatigue, and period flow together, since those are the symptoms tied most closely to the condition in the research above. What the Research Shows

Endometriosis is estimated to affect about 10% of women and girls of reproductive age worldwide, roughly 190 million people.[1]

Symptom severity doesn't reliably indicate how much disease is present, a pattern noted consistently enough that it's now considered a defining feature of the condition rather than an exception.[2]

The 17-study systematic review on diagnostic delay found wide variation by region: overall time to diagnosis ranged from about 5 to 8 years in the United States, United Kingdom, and Australia, and as high as a 12-year median in France, with the portion of that time between a first doctor's visit and confirmed diagnosis ranging from about 4 months to nearly 9 years across studies.[3]

Related Terms
