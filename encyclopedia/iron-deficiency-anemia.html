Iron-Deficiency Anemia

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Iron-deficiency anemia is a form of anemia that develops when the body's iron stores run too low to keep up with hemoglobin production, the protein red blood cells use to carry oxygen.[1] It's the most common cause of anemia worldwide, and it disproportionately affects people who menstruate and people who are pregnant, largely because of iron lost to menstrual bleeding and the added iron demands of pregnancy.[1][3]

What Iron-Deficiency Anemia Is

Researchers describe iron deficiency as a spectrum, not an on-off state. Stored iron declines first, then the iron available for building new red blood cells falls, and iron-deficiency anemia is the later stage where hemoglobin production has dropped enough to show up in the blood itself.[1] Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world, and the greatest prevalence of iron-deficiency anemia specifically falls on women of reproductive age.[2] A 2023 FIGO statement on the condition describes that burden as disproportionately borne by girls and women of reproductive age, tied mainly to menstrual and pregnancy-related iron loss.[3]

The symptoms researchers report most often are fatigue, shortness of breath on exertion, and paleness, features common to anemia generally.[1] Others show up more specifically alongside iron deficiency: hair thinning, brittle or ridged nails, pica (an urge to chew on ice or other non-food items), and restless legs. In one small study of 35 women with iron-deficiency anemia, 85.3% reported at least one of these non-anemia symptoms, most commonly diffuse hair loss and pica.[2]

Why People Track It

Menstrual blood loss is the leading cause of iron deficiency in women of reproductive age, and heavy menstrual bleeding specifically is a major, frequently normalized risk factor for it.[2][3] Our heavy menstrual bleeding entry covers that mechanism in more depth, including a 2024 study where menstrual blood loss independently predicted both hemoglobin and ferritin levels in premenopausal blood donors.[4] Flow and iron status are part of the same recurring physiology, which is why a period tracker touches iron at all.

People building a fuller picture of their iron status often follow a handful of related values together: ferritin (iron in reserve), hemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein itself), serum iron (what's circulating at the moment of a draw), and TIBC and transferrin saturation (how much of the blood's iron-carrying capacity is in use). On the symptom side, fatigue and restless legs are two of the daily-logged symptoms researchers connect to iron status.

What the Research Shows

The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 found the disability-adjusted life years attributable to iron deficiency rose between 1990 and 2021, with the highest burden concentrated in low socioeconomic areas, and prevalence among adolescent girls and women of reproductive age runs in the 9% to 11% range.[1]

The 2023 FIGO statement puts numbers on the mechanism: heavy menstrual bleeding may affect as many as half of reproductive-age women, and up to half of pregnant women who aren't yet anemic are already iron deficient by the first trimester.[3]

Menstrual blood loss was an independent, measurable determinant of ferritin and hemoglobin levels in a 2024 study of 473 premenopausal blood donors, on par with other established determinants of iron status.[4]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is iron-deficiency anemia the same thing as being low on iron?

They're related but not identical. Iron deficiency happens in stages: stored iron declines first, then the iron available for making new red blood cells falls, and iron-deficiency anemia is the later stage where hemoglobin production has dropped enough to affect the blood itself.[1] Someone can be iron deficient without yet being anemic.

Why is iron-deficiency anemia so common in people who menstruate?

Menstrual blood loss is the leading cause of iron deficiency among women of reproductive age, and heavy menstrual bleeding specifically is a major risk factor for it.[2][3] Pregnancy adds a second, separate source of iron demand on top of that.

Which labs do people commonly track for iron status?

Ferritin, hemoglobin, serum iron, and TIBC/transferrin saturation are the four values typically ordered together as an iron panel. Each reflects a different part of the picture: ferritin is stored iron, hemoglobin is the oxygen-carrying protein itself, serum iron is what's circulating at the moment of the draw, and TIBC/transferrin saturation describes how much of the blood's iron-carrying capacity is in use.
