Zinc

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Zinc is an essential trace mineral that acts as a catalyst in more than 300 enzymes and supports protein and DNA synthesis, wound healing, and normal taste.[1][2] The body keeps no dedicated zinc store, so it depends on regular dietary intake.[1] On lab panels it's measured as serum or plasma zinc from a blood draw.

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Zinc Measures

A zinc result on a lab report is the concentration of zinc circulating in your blood at the moment of the draw. Behind that number sits one of the busiest minerals in the body. More than 300 metalloenzymes use zinc as a catalyst, and roughly 2,500 transcription factors depend on it for their structure.[1] Zinc is also essential for building DNA, RNA, and proteins.[2] Its essentiality in humans was established in 1961.[3]

One thing sets zinc apart from a marker like ferritin: there's no functional reserve or body store of available zinc, so the body relies on a regular, adequate dietary supply rather than drawing down a stockpile.[1]

How It's Measured

Zinc is measured from a standard blood draw as serum or plasma zinc, usually reported in micrograms per deciliter.[1] Labs often group it with trace-mineral or micronutrient testing rather than a routine chemistry panel, and each lab reports its own reference interval with the result.

Serum zinc has honest limits as a marker. The body controls zinc homeostasis tightly, holding tissue concentrations steady across a wide range of intakes, so a blood level isn't a simple readout of what you eat.[1] Levels also shift with recent meals, time of day, and inflammation, and acute-phase responses can pull plasma zinc down in ways that have nothing to do with intake.[1][2] Even so, it remains the recommended status biomarker, the one measure of zinc status that can be used in individuals.[1]

Why People Track Zinc

Because the body can't bank zinc, status reflects recent and ongoing intake at the population level.[1] That makes it one of the nutritional markers people keep an eye on over time rather than treat as a one-off number. Zinc's day-to-day jobs are also unusually visible ones. It plays a role in every phase of wound healing, from coagulation through tissue repair and scar formation.[4] Impaired taste is a recognized feature of zinc deficiency in the clinical literature, which is why taste changes and zinc so often come up together.[2] A dated zinc result sits naturally next to logged nutrition and symptom data because they're parts of the same intake-and-effect picture.

What the Research Shows

The Biomarkers of Nutrition for Development (BOND) expert review found that whole-body zinc homeostasis is tightly controlled, that plasma zinc responds to severe and moderate dietary restriction, and that inflammation is among the factors that move plasma concentrations independent of intake.[1]

A 2018 review in Nutrients described zinc's regulatory role across each phase of wound healing, including coagulation, re-epithelialization, and scar formation.[4]

Clinical reference literature lists impaired taste among the recognized features of zinc deficiency, alongside zinc's core role in DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis.[2]

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia run zinc blood tests?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't draw blood or run labs. You get a zinc result from a blood draw ordered through a clinician or lab service, then upload it to your Go Go Gaia account so it's stored and charted alongside your other tracked data.

Why is a single serum zinc result hard to read on its own?

The body controls zinc levels tightly, so blood concentrations can hold fairly steady across a wide range of intakes. Levels also shift with recent meals, time of day, and inflammation. That's why a zinc result is usually read in context, and why your lab reports its own reference interval with each result.

Where does zinc show up on a lab report?

Zinc is usually reported as serum or plasma zinc, often grouped with trace-mineral or micronutrient testing rather than a routine chemistry panel. It's a separate measurement from minerals like serum iron, which has its own test and its own storage marker, ferritin.
