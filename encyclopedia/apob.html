ApoB (Apolipoprotein B)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Apolipoprotein B (ApoB) is the main structural protein of LDL, VLDL, and related lipoprotein particles, with one ApoB molecule on each particle.[1] Because of that one-to-one ratio, a blood ApoB level reflects the number of these particles in circulation.[2] It's measured with a standard blood draw and reported in milligrams per deciliter.

What ApoB Measures

ApoB is the protein scaffold that holds certain lipoprotein particles together, including LDL, VLDL, and chylomicrons, the packages that carry cholesterol and other fats through the blood.[1] Each of these particles carries exactly one molecule of ApoB.[1] That one-per-particle structure is what makes the test distinctive: an ApoB level works as a count of these particles, while an LDL cholesterol result measures the mass of cholesterol the particles carry.[2]

The two numbers can tell different stories in the same person, because the amount of cholesterol packed into each particle varies. Someone can carry a given amount of LDL cholesterol in many sparsely loaded particles or in fewer heavily loaded ones, so particle count and cholesterol mass don't always move together.[2]

How It's Measured

ApoB is measured from a standard venous blood draw using immunoassays standardized against WHO reference material, and results are reported in milligrams per deciliter.[1] It's not part of a routine lipid panel. Labs and clinicians treat it as an additional measurement, so it usually shows up on extended or advanced lipid panels rather than standard screening.[4][5]

Why People Track ApoB

ApoB has become a familiar name in health media, and people who order or receive extended lipid panels often want to keep the result somewhere useful. A joint consensus statement from two European laboratory and atherosclerosis societies describes ApoB as an additional measurement clinicians may use in cardiovascular risk assessment, alongside the standard cholesterol tests.[4] For someone tracking their health data, the practical draw is simpler: it's one more dated lab value that belongs in the same timeline as the rest of their lipid results.

What the Research Shows

The one-molecule-per-particle relationship is the settled biochemistry behind the test: each LDL, VLDL, and chylomicron particle carries a single ApoB molecule, so plasma ApoB concentration reflects the total number of these particles.[1][2]

A 2019 narrative review in JAMA Cardiology laid out why particle count and cholesterol mass can diverge: the cholesterol carried per particle varies between people, so ApoB and LDL cholesterol measure related but distinct quantities.[2]

In an analysis of the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, researchers found that when LDL cholesterol and LDL particle number disagreed in the same person, cardiovascular outcomes at the population level tracked with particle number.[3]

Related Terms

LDL Cholesterol

The mass of cholesterol carried inside LDL particles, where ApoB counts the particles.

HDL Cholesterol

Cholesterol carried in HDL particles, which don't carry ApoB.

Total Cholesterol

The summed cholesterol across all lipoprotein particles on a standard panel.

Lipoprotein(a)

An LDL-like particle with its own ApoB molecule plus an added apolipoprotein(a) tail.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is ApoB the same as LDL cholesterol?

No. LDL cholesterol measures the mass of cholesterol carried inside LDL particles, while ApoB counts the particles themselves, since each one carries a single ApoB molecule. The amount of cholesterol per particle varies from person to person, so the two numbers describe related but different things and don't always move together.

Is ApoB part of a standard cholesterol panel?

Usually not. A standard lipid panel reports total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, and triglycerides. ApoB is typically ordered separately or as part of an extended lipid panel, and lab guidance describes it as an additional measurement rather than routine screening.
