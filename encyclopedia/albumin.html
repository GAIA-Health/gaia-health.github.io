Albumin

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Albumin is the most abundant protein circulating in blood plasma, accounting for about half of total plasma protein.[1] Hepatocytes in the liver make it and release it directly into the bloodstream, where it helps hold fluid inside blood vessels and carries substances like bilirubin, fatty acids, and many medications through the body.[1]

What Albumin Measures

Albumin is built and shipped out fast. Hepatocytes, the liver's main working cells, synthesize it and release roughly 10 to 15 grams into the bloodstream every day, with almost none held back in storage.[1] Its two jobs follow from that abundance. As the biggest protein in the mix, albumin acts as the primary regulator of plasma oncotic pressure, the force that keeps fluid inside blood vessels instead of leaking into surrounding tissue.[1] It also works as a transport protein, binding and ferrying bilirubin, fatty acids, and a long list of drugs through the blood to wherever they're needed.[1]

Albumin also moves more slowly than a typical liver enzyme. Its long half-life means an albumin result reflects weeks of production and use rather than a single recent event, and its concentration is shaped by inflammation, nutrition, and how much fluid is in the bloodstream at the time of the draw, not liver function alone.[3]

How It's Measured

Albumin is measured from a standard venous blood draw and reported in grams per deciliter (g/dL). It's a routine part of the liver panel and the comprehensive metabolic panel, alongside ALT, AST, and bilirubin, and it's often reported next to total protein so a lab can calculate the albumin-to-globulin (A/G) ratio.[2][5] Each lab prints its own reference interval with the result.

Why People Track Albumin

Albumin shows up on almost any routine blood panel, so keeping a running record of it costs nothing extra beyond filing the number. Pregnancy gives it a women's-health angle most other liver-panel values don't have. As pregnancy advances, blood plasma volume expands substantially, and that added fluid dilutes the albumin already in circulation, so serum albumin concentration falls as a normal part of pregnancy.[4] Research on this pattern describes it as hemodilution: the body's total albumin mass and its rates of production and breakdown stay essentially unchanged, and it's the surrounding fluid volume that shifted.[4] A dated timeline is what makes that context visible, since a result from early pregnancy and one from the third trimester are answering a different question about the same protein.

What the Research Shows

Albumin's basic physiology is well documented: hepatocytes synthesize and release it at a rate of about 10 to 15 grams per day, and it functions as the primary driver of plasma oncotic pressure while transporting a wide range of endogenous and exogenous compounds.[1]

Because of its long half-life, albumin changes slowly compared with liver enzymes, and its concentration is influenced by inflammation, nutritional status, and fluid balance, in addition to liver synthetic function.[3]

Research on liver physiology in pregnancy attributes the fall in serum albumin across gestation to hemodilution from plasma volume expansion, noting that the intravascular mass of albumin and its synthesis and breakdown rates are not altered in normal pregnancy.[4]

Related Terms

Total Protein

The combined measure of albumin and globulins, reported on the same panel.

ALT

A liver enzyme measured on the same panel, but one that changes much faster than albumin.

Bilirubin

Another standard liver-panel value, from a pigment pathway rather than a protein one.

hCG

A pregnancy hormone tracked across the same trimesters that reshape plasma volume.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia run albumin blood tests?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't draw blood or run labs. You get an albumin result from a blood draw ordered through a clinician or lab service, then upload it to your Go Go Gaia account so it's stored and charted alongside your other tracked data.

What's the difference between albumin and total protein?

Total protein adds up every protein circulating in blood plasma. Albumin is one piece of that total, typically the largest single piece, and the rest is a group of proteins called globulins. Labs report albumin and total protein as separate values on the same panel.

Why does albumin drop during a normal pregnancy?

Pregnancy expands blood plasma volume substantially, and that extra fluid dilutes the albumin already circulating in it. Research describes this as hemodilution, a change in concentration rather than a change in how much albumin the body is actually making or breaking down.
