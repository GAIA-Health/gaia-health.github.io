Audio Exposure

Biometrics & Vitals · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Audio exposure covers two related HealthKit measurements: headphone audio exposure, the loudness of sound played through connected headphones, and environmental audio exposure, the ambient sound level an Apple Watch's microphone detects around the wearer. Both are logged in decibels over time rather than as a single reading.[4]

What Audio Exposure Measures

These are two separate metrics that happen to share a unit. Headphone audio exposure is about what's playing directly into your ears. Environmental audio exposure is about the sound level in the room or space around you, independent of whether you're wearing headphones at all. Both are recorded in A-weighted decibels, a scale adjusted to roughly match how sensitive human hearing is at different frequencies.[2]

How It's Measured

Headphone audio exposure is calculated automatically when compatible headphones are connected to an iPhone. Apple notes that measurements are most accurate with Apple or Beats headphones, since those can report volume data directly, while other headphones are estimated from the device's own volume setting.[4] Environmental audio exposure works differently: it comes from the Noise app on an Apple Watch, which uses the Watch's built-in microphone to sample ambient sound levels intermittently throughout the day and sends readings to the Health app.

Why People Track Audio Exposure

Hearing health from everyday listening has become a bigger public-health focus as personal audio devices have become constant companions. The World Health Organization's 2021 World Report on Hearing put a number on the scale of the issue: over 1 billion young adults worldwide are at risk of permanent, avoidable hearing loss due to unsafe listening practices.[1] That's the backdrop for why headphone and environmental sound level are logged as their own metrics rather than left untracked.

What the Research Shows

The underlying physiology is well established. Noise-induced hearing loss happens through two related pathways: an intense, immediate sound event can damage the outer hair cells in the cochlea, while chronic exposure over time produces a slower, cumulative process that also degrades outer hair cells and the connecting auditory nerve. Because human sensory hair cells don't regenerate once lost, that kind of damage is permanent, and it's compounded by oxidative stress and reduced blood flow within the inner ear during prolonged exposure.[2]

In 2019, WHO and the International Telecommunication Union jointly published a technical standard for personal audio devices, describing a "sound allowance" system in which a device tracks a listener's cumulative exposure as a percentage of a reference level and surfaces that back to the user as an individualized listening profile, rather than simply capping volume outright.[3] That standard is aimed at how manufacturers design devices and what information they give listeners, not a personal target for any one listener to hit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between headphone audio and environmental audio exposure?

Headphone audio exposure is the loudness of sound coming through connected headphones, most accurate with Apple or Beats headphones and otherwise estimated from your device's volume setting. Environmental audio exposure is the ambient sound level around you, sampled by the microphone on an Apple Watch running the Noise app. One measures what's in your ears, the other measures the room or environment you're in.

Does Go Go Gaia tell me if my audio exposure is unsafe?

No. Go Go Gaia charts the headphone and environmental audio exposure levels your iPhone and Apple Watch already log through Apple Health. It doesn't set a decibel limit or flag your listening as safe or unsafe.

What is the WHO doing about unsafe listening?

The World Health Organization estimates that unsafe listening practices put a very large number of young people worldwide at risk of avoidable hearing loss. Alongside the International Telecommunication Union, WHO published a technical standard for how personal audio devices can track a listener's cumulative sound exposure and surface that information back to the user.
