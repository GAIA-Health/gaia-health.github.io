Cold Intolerance

Symptoms · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Cold intolerance is an unusual sensitivity to a cold environment or cold temperatures.[1] Someone with it feels cold when the people around them are comfortable, or needs extra layers to feel warm. It's a reported sensation rather than a temperature reading, so it's logged as a symptom, not measured by a device.

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Cold Intolerance Describes

How warm you feel comes down to thermoregulation. The hypothalamus works as the body's thermostat, holding core temperature near 37°C by balancing heat loss against heat production. In the cold it triggers vasoconstriction and shivering, and it raises metabolic rate through thyroid hormone release, which increases heat production.[2] Cold intolerance describes the experience of that balance sitting low: feeling cold in conditions most people find comfortable. MedlinePlus notes it can be a symptom of a problem with metabolism, and that having very little body fat also makes cold harder to tolerate.[1]

How It's Recorded

There's no sensor for it. You log the days you felt unusually cold, as a simple yes-or-no or with a note. Measured temperature metrics like wrist temperature are separate data that can sit next to those logs.

Why People Track Cold Intolerance

A pattern over weeks is more useful to describe than a single cold afternoon, so people log it to see whether it's occasional or constant, and whether it moves with their cycle, sleep, or eating. Some people who get thyroid panels drawn chart their cold-intolerance logs alongside lab results like TSH and free T4.

What the Research Shows

A Physiological Reviews paper on thyroid hormone and metabolism shows the hormone maintains basal metabolic rate and drives adaptive thermogenesis, the extra heat production the body switches on in the cold. In animal studies, low thyroid hormone led to marked drops in body temperature during cold exposure, and treatment reversed it.[3]

Clinical references list cold intolerance among the classic features described in hypothyroidism research, where the characteristic lab findings are elevated TSH and low free T4.[4] That's a population-level description of where the symptom appears in the literature, not a statement about what any one person's cold intolerance means.

Related Terms

TSH

The pituitary hormone measured in thyroid panels that people chart symptom logs against.

Free T4

The unbound thyroid hormone measured in blood, a driver of metabolic rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is cold intolerance the same as feeling cold?

No. Everyone feels cold in a cold room. Cold intolerance describes an unusual sensitivity to cold, like feeling cold when the people around you are comfortable, or needing far more layers than usual to feel warm.[1]

How is cold intolerance measured?

It isn't measured by a device. Cold intolerance is a reported sensation, so it's recorded as a symptom log: you note the days you felt unusually cold. Body temperature readings like wrist temperature are separate, measured metrics that can sit alongside those logs.

Why does metabolism affect how warm you feel?

Heat is a by-product of metabolism. Thyroid hormone helps set the body's basal metabolic rate, and research shows it drives heat production both at baseline and during cold exposure.[3] The hypothalamus balances that heat production against heat loss to hold core temperature near 37°C.[2]
