Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal condition, and researchers describe it as the most common endocrine disorder among women of reproductive age worldwide.[1] It's defined by some combination of irregular ovulation, elevated androgen levels, and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound, and clinicians diagnose it using the Rotterdam criteria, which require at least two of those three features.[3]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What PCOS Is

Researchers and clinicians diagnose PCOS using the Rotterdam criteria, which require at least two of three features: infrequent or absent ovulation, hyperandrogenism (elevated androgen levels shown clinically or on a blood test), and polycystic ovarian morphology seen on ultrasound.[3] The framework is built to rule other things out first, since conditions like thyroid disorders can produce a similar picture, and no single feature on its own defines the condition since any one of the three can appear without the other two.[1]

PCOS is also tied to metabolic changes beyond the reproductive system. Insulin resistance is estimated to affect around 70% of women with PCOS, independent of body weight,[1] and researchers describe androgen excess and insulin resistance as reinforcing each other: higher insulin is linked to more androgen output from the ovaries, while androgen excess is linked to lower insulin sensitivity.[1],[4] The World Health Organization notes PCOS carries a longer-term association with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.[2]

Why People With PCOS Track These Metrics

Because PCOS is defined by a combination of features that each move on their own timeline, people who have it often log more than one signal instead of relying on any single symptom. Cycle length is one of the more visible ones: researchers define irregularity in this context with specific thresholds, for example fewer than eight cycles a year, or cycle lengths outside 21 to 35 days more than three years past a first period, one of three Rotterdam features researchers assess together rather than a standalone indicator.[3] A dated cycle-length history is what turns an impression of unpredictable periods into a pattern that's actually measurable. See our irregular periods entry for more on how cycle variability is defined.

Androgen-related changes are the other visible signal, and they often show up as physical symptoms before any lab work happens: hirsutism, coarse hair growth in androgen-sensitive areas, and hormonal acne are both driven by the same androgen pathway.[1] The lab-based version of that same signal is testosterone, measured as total or free testosterone to check for elevated androgen levels alongside the physical signs.[3]

Because insulin resistance is so common in PCOS, insulin and related glucose markers are frequently logged alongside cycle and androgen data, since the three systems tend to move together rather than independently.[1][4] Our PCOS guide goes deeper on how these pieces fit together.

What the Research Shows

PCOS affects an estimated 10% to 13% of women of reproductive age worldwide by the World Health Organization's estimate, though reported prevalence ranges wider, from about 5% to 26%, depending on which diagnostic criteria a study uses.[2][1] The WHO also estimates that up to 70% of women with PCOS worldwide are undiagnosed.[2]

Insulin resistance is estimated to affect around 70% of women with PCOS and operates independently of body weight,[1] and elevated insulin and elevated androgens are described as reinforcing each other in a self-sustaining cycle.[1],[4]

The international guideline behind the current Rotterdam-based diagnostic framework also notes that women internationally report delayed diagnosis and dissatisfaction with the diagnostic process, part of the reasoning behind a structured diagnostic algorithm rather than reliance on any single test.[3]

Related Terms

Irregular Periods

Cycle-to-cycle length variability, one of the features the Rotterdam criteria weighs.

Hirsutism

Coarse hair growth in androgen-sensitive areas, a clinical sign of hyperandrogenism.

Hormonal Acne

Breakouts driven by the same androgen pathway as hirsutism.

Testosterone

The lab marker used to check for biochemical hyperandrogenism.

Insulin

The pancreatic hormone linked to PCOS through insulin resistance.
