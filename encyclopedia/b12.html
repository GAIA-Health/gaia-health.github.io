Vitamin B12

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Vitamin B12, also called cobalamin, is a water-soluble vitamin built around a cobalt atom.[1][5] The body needs it to make DNA and form red blood cells.[2] It's measured with a standard blood draw, reported in picograms per milliliter, and often ordered alongside folate on the same panel.

What Vitamin B12 Does

B12 is built around a metal atom, cobalt, at its core.[1] Cells use it in DNA synthesis, which is why fast-dividing cells like the ones that become red blood cells depend on a steady supply.[1][2] B12 also serves as the cofactor for methionine synthase, the enzyme that recycles homocysteine back into methionine. That reaction is where B12's chemistry meets folate's, since folate donates the methyl group the enzyme transfers.[2]

Absorbing B12 takes an unusual relay. The stomach's parietal cells secrete a glycoprotein called intrinsic factor, which binds B12 and carries it through the gut until receptors in the last section of the small intestine, the terminal ileum, pull the complex in.[3] No other vitamin depends on a dedicated stomach-made escort like this, and it's why B12 absorption involves the stomach even though uptake happens far downstream.[3]

How It's Measured

Serum B12 comes from a standard venous blood draw and is usually reported in picograms per milliliter. Each lab prints its own reference interval next to the result, and intervals vary by lab and testing method. Researchers have also documented limits to serum B12 as a stand-alone marker, because much of what it counts is B12 bound to a protein that cells can't take up.[4] Methylmalonic acid and holotranscobalamin are additional markers studied as ways to describe B12 status from other angles.[4]

Why People Track Vitamin B12

B12 shows up on routine bloodwork, anemia workups, and prenatal panels, so many people end up with results from different labs across different years. Diet is one reason the number draws attention at the population level: B12 occurs naturally in animal foods like meat, fish, eggs, and dairy, so intake patterns differ in people eating plant-based diets, and nutrition researchers study that group's B12 status closely.[2][5] Tracking results over time turns those scattered printouts into one series you can actually see.

What the Research Shows

B12's place in DNA synthesis is well established: it participates in reactions vital for cell division and growth, which is what links the vitamin to red blood cell production.[1][2]

The intrinsic factor mechanism has been mapped in detail, from secretion by gastric parietal cells to receptor-mediated uptake of the B12 complex in the terminal ileum.[3]

A 2016 review of B12 biomarkers concluded that total serum B12 isn't a reliable marker of B12 status when used alone, and described methylmalonic acid and holotranscobalamin as the markers researchers use to sharpen the picture.[4]

Related Terms

Folate

The B vitamin that partners with B12 in DNA synthesis, often tested on the same panel.

Homocysteine

The amino acid that B12-dependent chemistry recycles back into methionine.

Red Blood Cells

The fast-dividing cell line whose production depends on B12 and folate.

Vitamin D

Another vitamin measured by blood draw and tracked as a lab result over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Go Go Gaia run vitamin B12 blood tests?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't draw blood or run labs. You get a B12 result from a blood draw ordered through a clinician or lab service, then upload it to your Go Go Gaia account so it's stored and charted alongside your other tracked data.

What's the difference between vitamin B12 and folate?

Both are B vitamins involved in DNA synthesis, and their metabolic pathways meet at the same reaction that recycles homocysteine into methionine. They're separate lab tests, though, and labs often order them together because the two vitamins work as partners in that shared chemistry.

Why do lab reports sometimes list methylmalonic acid next to B12?

Research on B12 testing has shown that total serum B12 has limits as a stand-alone marker, because most of the B12 it counts is bound to a protein that cells can't take up. Methylmalonic acid and holotranscobalamin are additional markers labs can measure to describe B12 status from other angles.
