Spotting & Intermenstrual Bleeding

Cycle & Fertility · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Spotting is light vaginal bleeding that happens between menstrual periods, outside the days counted as a period.[1] Researchers call it intermenstrual bleeding. Trackers log it separately from period flow, as a dated entry, so a spotting day doesn't reset the cycle-day count that starts each new cycle.

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Spotting Describes

Spotting describes bleeding by its timing, not its cause. Any vaginal bleeding that falls between one period and the next counts as intermenstrual bleeding.[1] The FIGO system that standardizes how researchers describe bleeding symptoms treats intermenstrual bleeding as its own named category, separate from questions about how heavy, long, or frequent periods themselves are.[2]

How It's Logged

Spotting isn't measured with a device. It's a dated log entry: the day it happened, kept separate from period flow. That separation matters for the day count, because day 1 of a cycle is the first day of a real period, not a spotting day. Logged this way, each entry lands on a specific cycle day, which is what makes patterns visible later. Our cycle length entry covers how that day counting works.

Why People Track Spotting

One spotting day is just a data point. Months of them answer better questions: does it cluster around the same cycle day, did it start after a contraception change, or is it scattered at random? That timing picture is something only a log can build, and it's also concrete information to bring to any conversation about your cycle. If you're comparing spotting near an expected period with an actual period, our guide to implantation bleeding vs. a period covers how the two patterns differ.

What the Research Shows

Mid-cycle bleeding shows up in population data, but not often. In a study of 201 regularly menstruating women followed across 470 cycles, 4.8% of women experienced mid-cycle bleeding.[3]

Contraception is the other well-documented context. Nearly 20% of women using contraception use progestin-only methods like pills, injections, implants, and hormonal IUDs, and a review in the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology describes unscheduled bleeding and spotting as a common experience with these methods and a known reason people stop using them.[4]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What's the difference between spotting and a period?

Timing and volume. A period is the days of menstrual flow that start a cycle, and day 1 of the cycle is the first day of that flow. Spotting is lighter bleeding that shows up outside those days.[1] Trackers keep the two separate so a spotting day doesn't get counted as the start of a new cycle.

Is mid-cycle spotting common?

Population data suggests it's the exception rather than the rule. In a study of 201 regularly menstruating women followed across 470 cycles, 4.8% of women experienced mid-cycle bleeding.[3]

Why log spotting separately from period flow?

One spotting day means little on its own. Logged over months, spotting entries show whether it clusters at a particular point in your cycle, lines up with a contraception change, or happens at random. Keeping it separate from period flow also protects the cycle-day count, since day 1 should only reset when a real period starts.
