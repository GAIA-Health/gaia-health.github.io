Sugar & Added Sugar

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Total sugar is every simple sugar in a food, whether it occurs naturally, like the fructose in fruit or the lactose in milk, or was added during processing or preparation.[2] Added sugar is that second part measured on its own: sugars and syrups put into food when it's made, not sugars the food already contained.[2] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks both in grams from the meals you log.

What Sugar & Added Sugar Mean

Sugar is a simple carbohydrate, one or two sugar units bonded together, which is what puts it in the same family as the glucose your body uses for fuel.[3] "Total sugar" on a food label adds up every simple sugar in that food regardless of where it came from.

"Added sugar" narrows that down to just the sugars and caloric sweeteners that were put in during processing or preparation, things like brown sugar, cane sugar, corn syrup, honey, and high-fructose corn syrup, and explicitly excludes sugars a food already had, like the fructose naturally in fruit or the lactose naturally in milk.[2] The World Health Organization uses a related but broader term, "free sugars," which covers added sugars plus the sugars in honey, syrups, fruit juice, and fruit juice concentrates, while still excluding sugars intact inside whole fruit, vegetables, and milk.[1][4] The practical distinction in both definitions is the same: sugar built into a whole food's own structure is treated differently from sugar poured in or extracted out.

Why People Track Sugar & Added Sugar

WHO's 2015 guideline recommends reducing free sugars intake to less than 10% of total energy intake, with a further reduction below 5% offering additional benefit, based on evidence linking free sugars to dental caries and to unhealthy weight gain.[1] That's a population-level guideline from a public health body, not a personal target, which is part of why some people want to see added sugar broken out separately from total sugar rather than combined into a single number.

What the Research Shows

The WHO guideline's 10% threshold for free sugars draws on moderate-quality evidence from observational studies of dental caries, while the additional 5% threshold rests on very-low-quality ecological evidence showing a dose-response relationship at lower intake levels, and the guideline notes no identified harms from getting below 5%.[1]

On the labeling side, the U.S. definition of added sugars specifically carves out sugars naturally present in fruit and milk, so two foods with identical total sugar can have very different added sugar values depending on where that sugar came from.[2]

Structurally, sugar sits within the broader carbohydrate classification as a simple carbohydrate, one or two bonded sugar units, distinct from the longer chains that make up starches and fiber.[3]

What's the difference between "sugar" and "added sugar" on a nutrition label?

Total sugar is every simple sugar in a food, whether it's naturally there, like the fructose in an apple, or was added while the food was made. Added sugar is that second part isolated on its own: sugars and syrups put in during processing or preparation, not sugars the food already contained. A plain apple has sugar but no added sugar. A sweetened apple sauce has both.

Does the WHO have a recommendation on sugar intake?

Yes. The World Health Organization's 2015 guideline recommends reducing free sugars, a category that includes added sugars plus sugars in honey, syrups, and fruit juice, to less than 10% of total energy intake, with an additional benefit from getting below 5%. That's a population-level guideline, not personalized advice, and Go Go Gaia doesn't set individual sugar targets.

Yes. Go Go Gaia's food logger breaks each meal down into both total sugar and added sugar as distinct values, so they show up separately in your nutrition data rather than combined into one number.
