Protein (Dietary)

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Dietary protein is one of the three macronutrients, built from amino acids, nine of which the body can't make on its own and has to get from food.[1] Digestion breaks it down into individual amino acids, which are then used to build and repair tissue and make hormones and other molecules.[1] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks it in grams from the meals you log.

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Dietary Protein Is

Protein shows up in a wide range of foods, including eggs, meat, dairy, seafood, legumes, nuts, and seeds.[1] Nine of the amino acids that make it up are essential, meaning the body can't synthesize them and depends on food to supply them.[1] Animal proteins, along with soy, contain all nine, and a cup of tofu has about the same amount of protein as three ounces of steak, chicken, or fish.[1]

Digestion takes protein apart in stages. Stomach acid unfolds it so the enzyme pepsin can cleave it into smaller chains, then pancreatic enzymes break those chains down further into tripeptides, dipeptides, and individual amino acids, which get absorbed and used to build and repair tissue.[1]

Why People Track Protein

Protein intake doesn't stay flat across the menstrual cycle for everyone. A small study of 23 regularly menstruating women found protein intake was higher in the luteal phase than the follicular phase, 1.17 versus 1.09 grams per kilogram of bodyweight, with the difference driven mainly by the less physically active group.[2] It's one modest study rather than an established pattern, but it lines up with the broader appetite shift many people notice in the days before their period. For more on what else happens during that window, see our guide to the luteal phase.

What the Research Shows

Nine amino acids are classified as essential because the body has no pathway to build them itself, which is why dietary sources of complete protein matter for supplying them.[1]

Protein digestion is a multi-step process: hydrochloric acid in the stomach denatures the protein, pepsin cleaves it into oligopeptide chains, and pancreatic enzymes finish the job in the duodenum, breaking those chains into the tripeptides, dipeptides, and single amino acids that get absorbed.[1]

In the cycle study, the higher luteal-phase protein intake showed up specifically in the physically inactive group, with no statistically significant phase difference in the more active group, so it isn't a uniform effect across all women.[2]

Does protein intake really change across the menstrual cycle?

One small study of 23 women found protein intake was higher in the luteal phase than the follicular phase, both as a percentage of calories and in grams per kilogram of bodyweight, though the effect was strongest in the less physically active group. It's a single study, not a guideline, but it's consistent with the general uptick in appetite and food intake many people notice before their period.

What makes a protein source "complete"?

A complete protein supplies all nine essential amino acids, the ones the body can't build on its own and has to get from food. Animal proteins like eggs, dairy, meat, and seafood are complete on their own, and so is soy. Other plant proteins can be combined across a day's meals to cover the full set.
