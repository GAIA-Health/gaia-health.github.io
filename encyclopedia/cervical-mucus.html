Cervical Mucus

Cycle & Fertility Terms · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Cervical mucus is a fluid secreted by glands in the cervix that changes in volume, texture, and stretchiness across the menstrual cycle as estrogen and progesterone levels shift.[1] Near ovulation it typically becomes clear, slippery, and stretchy; after ovulation it thickens again.[2]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Cervical Mucus Measures

Cervical mucus is produced by cervical crypts, glandular structures in the cervix wall that increase their output as estrogen rises.[1] In the days before ovulation, rising estrogen makes the mucus thinner, clearer, and stretchier: the classic egg-white texture that's easier for sperm to move through. During this window it also shows a characteristic ferning pattern under a microscope.[2] After ovulation, progesterone from the corpus luteum reverses these changes, and the mucus becomes thicker, more opaque, and lower in volume.[2]

How It's Measured

Cervical mucus needs no device or lab draw. You observe it directly, typically at the vulva or vaginal opening, and describe it by sensation (dry, moist, wet, slippery) and appearance (none, sticky, creamy, watery, or egg-white). The estrogenic, egg-white-type mucus associated with peak fertility generally appears in the few days leading up to ovulation and is gone shortly after.[2]

Why People Track Cervical Mucus

Cervical mucus is one of the few fertility signs that changes in the days leading up to ovulation rather than only confirming it afterward, which is why it's central to mucus-based fertility awareness methods. A study of women observing their own mucus patterns found that day-specific mucus observations predicted the fertile window and conception better than intercourse timing based on calendar calculations alone.[3] People also log it simply to understand their own cycle pattern over time, alongside other cycle-phase signs.

What the Research Shows

A pooled analysis of 2,488 ovulatory cycles across three cohorts found that women averaged 6.4 days of peak, estrogenic-type mucus and 12.1 potentially fertile days per cycle. Both figures varied by age and parity: nulliparous women aged 30 and older showed less mucus production and fewer potentially fertile days than younger nulliparous women.[4]

A review of ovulation-detection methods notes that mucus observation is among the lowest-cost approaches available, requiring no equipment, and describes it as an effective way to identify the periovulatory window when done consistently.[5]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What does egg-white cervical mucus mean?

Egg-white cervical mucus is the clear, stretchy, slippery mucus that appears in the days leading up to ovulation, when estrogen is at its highest. Researchers describe it as the peak-fertility mucus type, and population studies show it clusters around a roughly six-day window ending near ovulation.[4]

Does cervical mucus change after ovulation?

Yes. After ovulation, progesterone from the corpus luteum causes cervical mucus to thicken and become scant again, reversing the estrogen-driven changes seen in the days before ovulation.[2]

Why do fertility awareness methods use cervical mucus?

Cervical mucus is one of the few fertility signs that changes before ovulation rather than only confirming it afterward, which is why it's a core observation in mucus-based fertility awareness methods alongside signs like basal body temperature.[2]
