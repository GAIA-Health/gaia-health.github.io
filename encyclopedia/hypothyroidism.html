Hypothyroidism

Conditions & Life Stages · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Hypothyroidism is a condition in which the thyroid gland doesn't produce enough thyroid hormone to meet the body's needs.[1] In the most common form, falling thyroid hormone triggers the pituitary gland to release more TSH, and in the United States, autoimmune thyroid disease, known as Hashimoto thyroiditis, is the most common underlying cause.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Hypothyroidism Is

The thyroid is a small gland at the base of the neck that produces thyroid hormone, mainly thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which influence metabolism throughout the body. In primary hypothyroidism, the most common form, the thyroid gland itself underproduces these hormones, and the drop in circulating thyroid hormone reduces the negative feedback signal that normally keeps the pituitary gland in check, so the pituitary releases more TSH to try to stimulate the thyroid.[1] That's the mechanism our TSH entry covers in more detail. In the United States, autoimmune thyroid disease, known as Hashimoto thyroiditis, is the most common underlying cause of hypothyroidism.[1]

Documented symptoms of hypothyroidism include fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, dry skin, hair loss, constipation, muscle aches, voice changes, and depressed mood, and a slowed heart rate is a commonly documented exam finding.[1] None of these symptoms are unique to hypothyroidism on their own, which is part of why researchers rely on lab measurement rather than symptoms alone to describe the condition.

Why People Track These Metrics Alongside Hypothyroidism

TSH is the most frequently tracked lab marker in this context, since it sits at the top of the feedback loop described above and moves in the opposite direction from thyroid hormone itself. See our TSH entry for more on what it measures and how it's tested. Free T4 and free T3, the unbound, active forms of the thyroid gland's own hormones, are the other labs typically drawn alongside TSH to build a fuller picture of thyroid output.

Because hypothyroidism's symptoms overlap with common, nonspecific complaints, people track them as their own daily entries rather than relying on memory of how a week felt. See our fatigue and cold intolerance entries for how each is logged.

Hypothyroidism is also documented in connection with menstrual cycle changes. A case-control study of people with hypothyroidism found 23.4% reported irregular cycles, mainly oligomenorrhea (infrequent periods) and heavy menstrual bleeding, compared with 8.4% of people without a thyroid disorder.[4] A broader research review of thyroid function and reproductive health describes hypothyroidism as associated mainly with oligomenorrhea.[3] People exploring a possible link between thyroid symptoms and cycle changes often log cycle length and flow alongside thyroid labs and symptoms. What the Research Shows

The NHANES III population survey found an overall hypothyroidism prevalence of 4.6% in the US population studied (0.3% clinical, 4.3% subclinical), and both mean TSH and the prevalence of thyroid antibodies, markers of autoimmune thyroid disease, were higher in women than in men.[2]

A case-control study of 171 people with hypothyroidism found 23.4% reported menstrual disturbances, mainly oligomenorrhea and heavy menstrual bleeding, compared with 8.4% of 214 people without a thyroid disorder.[4]

A broader review of thyroid function and reproductive health describes hypothyroidism as associated mainly with oligomenorrhea, part of a wider body of research connecting thyroid hormone to reproductive-system function.[3]

Related Terms

TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone)

The pituitary hormone measured in nearly every thyroid panel, rising as thyroid hormone falls.

Free T4

The thyroid gland's main hormone, typically drawn alongside TSH to see how the gland is performing.

Free T3

The active thyroid hormone most tissues actually use, sometimes added to a fuller thyroid panel.

Fatigue

One of hypothyroidism's most commonly documented symptoms, logged as a daily rating.

Cold Intolerance

A documented hypothyroidism symptom, logged as a felt sensation rather than a measured temperature.

Hashimoto's Thyroiditis

The autoimmune condition that's the most common underlying cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient countries.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is hypothyroidism?

In the most common form, falling thyroid hormone triggers the pituitary gland to release more TSH, and in the United States, autoimmune thyroid disease, known as Hashimoto thyroiditis, is the most common underlying cause.[1]

Why is hypothyroidism described as more common in women?

Population research has found that markers linked to hypothyroidism run higher in women than men. In the NHANES III survey of the US population, both mean TSH and the prevalence of thyroid antibodies, markers of autoimmune thyroid disease, were higher in women than in men.[2]

Can hypothyroidism affect the menstrual cycle?

Research has documented an association between hypothyroidism and menstrual cycle disturbances. A case-control study found 23.4% of people with hypothyroidism reported irregular cycles, mainly oligomenorrhea and heavy menstrual bleeding, compared with 8.4% of people without a thyroid disorder, and a broader research review describes hypothyroidism as associated mainly with oligomenorrhea.[3][4]
