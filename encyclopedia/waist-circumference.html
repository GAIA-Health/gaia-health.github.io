Waist Circumference

Body Composition & Fitness · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Waist circumference is a tape-measure reading taken around the abdomen, used as an anthropometric marker of central, or abdominal, adiposity. It's measured following a standardized protocol, a horizontal tape at a defined landmark, so results stay comparable from one measurement to the next.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Waist Circumference Measures

Waist circumference is an essential anthropometric measurement because it directly reflects central adiposity, the fat carried around the abdomen, in a way that overall body weight or BMI doesn't capture on their own.[1] Two people at the same weight can carry that weight differently, and waist circumference is tracked as a separate number precisely because it captures where fat sits rather than just how much of it there is.

How It's Measured

The standard protocol, used by NHANES and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, starts with locating a bony landmark: the examiner finds the uppermost lateral border of the iliac crest, the top of the hip bone, and marks that point.[2] A measuring tape is then placed in a horizontal plane around the abdomen at that marked level, kept parallel to the floor, snug against the skin but not compressing it, and read at the end of a normal breath.[3] Following the same landmark and tape placement every time is what makes repeated measurements comparable to each other.

Why People Track Waist Circumference

Because central adiposity behaves differently in the body than fat carried elsewhere, some people track waist circumference as its own trend, separate from total body weight, to see how that specific measurement moves over time. What the Research Shows

StatPearls describes waist circumference as an essential anthropometric measurement because it directly captures central adiposity, distinct from weight-based measures like BMI, which don't distinguish where on the body fat is carried.[1]

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute's measurement protocol, developed for its clinical evidence report on obesity, specifies the exact landmark and tape placement used to take the measurement, precisely so that a reading taken by one examiner on one day can be meaningfully compared to a reading taken by someone else later on.[2] The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics uses a closely related protocol in its ongoing National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, again built around a horizontal tape at the iliac crest read at the end of normal breathing.[3]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

What other circumferences does Go Go Gaia let me log besides waist?

Hip, chest, arm, thigh, calf, and neck circumference are all separate logged metrics in Go Go Gaia. Each is its own dated data point, tracked the same way as waist circumference, a tape measurement logged over time.

Why does it matter where on the body I measure?

Waist circumference is measured at a specific landmark, generally the uppermost point of the hip bone, so that the number means the same thing every time it's taken. Measuring at a different point, or with the tape angled instead of level, changes the reading even if nothing about the body has actually changed.

Does Go Go Gaia calculate a waist-to-hip ratio?

Go Go Gaia logs waist and hip circumference as separate metrics, each charted as its own trend over time. It doesn't compute or display a combined ratio between the two.
