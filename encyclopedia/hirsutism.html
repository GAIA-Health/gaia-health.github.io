Hirsutism

Symptoms & Logged Metrics · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Hirsutism is the growth of coarse, pigmented terminal hair in body areas where hair growth depends on androgens, such as the face, chest, and abdomen.[1] It reflects the interaction between circulating androgen levels and how sensitive each hair follicle is to those hormones.[1]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Hirsutism Reflects

Hair follicles come in two broad types: small vellus follicles that make fine, nearly invisible hair, and terminal follicles that make thicker, pigmented hair. Androgens can switch a follicle from the first type to the second, and the response depends on the body site because it's built into the follicle itself.[2] Within the skin, follicles convert testosterone into dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a more potent androgen, using the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase. Both the skin and its follicles carry androgen receptors, so follicle sensitivity matters as much as the hormone level in the blood.[1]

How It's Measured

Day to day, hirsutism is a logged observation, not a lab value. You note where coarse hair grows and whether it's changing over time. In clinical practice, the Endocrine Society's guideline treats hirsutism as one of the findings that can prompt a doctor to test androgen levels such as testosterone.[3] A symptom log can't diagnose anything, but it gives that conversation a dated record to work from.

Why People Track Hirsutism

Coarse-hair growth changes slowly, over months rather than days, and memory is a poor instrument for that. A dated log shows whether things are stable, increasing, or easing. Hirsutism also sits in the same androgen family as hormonal acne and hair thinning, so people who log one often log all three next to hormone results like testosterone, free testosterone, and SHBG.

What the Research Shows

Hirsutism is common. A consensus statement by the Androgen Excess and PCOS Society puts its prevalence at roughly 10% of women in most populations, with variation across ethnic backgrounds.[4]

The follicle's response to androgens is site-specific. The same hormones that push terminal-hair growth in areas like the face and chest can have no effect, or the opposite effect, on follicles elsewhere on the body.[2]

Hirsutism can also occur when measured androgen levels sit in the usual range. Researchers attribute these cases to heightened follicle sensitivity rather than raised hormones.[1]

Related Terms

Testosterone

The main circulating androgen, converted in hair follicles to the more potent DHT.

Free Testosterone

The unbound fraction of testosterone that's available to act on tissues like hair follicles.

SHBG

The carrier protein that binds testosterone in the blood and shapes how much circulates free.

Hormonal Acne

Another androgen-responsive skin sign, driven by sebaceous glands rather than hair follicles.

Hair Thinning

Reduced scalp hair density, where androgens can act in the opposite direction to hirsutism.

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is hirsutism the same as having a lot of body hair?

No. Hirsutism refers specifically to coarse, pigmented terminal hair growing in androgen-dependent areas like the face, chest, and abdomen.[1] Fine, light vellus hair over the body is a separate thing, and overall hair amount varies a lot between people and ethnic backgrounds.[4]

Which hormones are involved in hirsutism?

Androgens. Hair follicles convert testosterone into the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT) using the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase, and both the skin and its follicles carry androgen receptors. Follicle sensitivity matters as much as hormone levels, so hirsutism can occur even when measured androgens are in the usual range.[1]

Why do doctors sometimes order hormone tests for hirsutism?

Clinical guidelines from the Endocrine Society treat hirsutism as one of the signs that can prompt testing of androgen levels, such as testosterone.[3] That evaluation is a clinical decision made with a doctor, not something a symptom log can do on its own.
