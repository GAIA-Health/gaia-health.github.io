eGFR (Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate)

Kidney Labs · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

eGFR, the estimated glomerular filtration rate, describes how fast the kidneys filter blood. It isn't measured directly. A lab computes it from a serum creatinine blood test plus age and sex, using a standard equation. Most labs report it automatically whenever a metabolic panel includes creatinine.[2][5]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What eGFR Measures

Each kidney filters blood through tiny bundles of capillaries called glomeruli. Glomerular filtration rate is the volume of plasma that flows through those filters in a set stretch of time.[1] The scale is bigger than most people expect. Healthy kidneys filter roughly 180 liters of plasma a day, then reclaim almost all of it, leaving about a liter and a half as urine.[1]

How It's Estimated

eGFR isn't a test of its own. The lab measures creatinine, a waste product of muscle metabolism that the kidneys filter out of the blood.[2] An equation then combines that creatinine value with your age and sex to estimate the filtration rate, reported in mL/min/1.73m².[2] The current standard is the CKD-EPI equation, which researchers updated in 2021 to work from creatinine, age, and sex without a race term.[3]

Why People Track eGFR

Mostly because it arrives on its own. The National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology recommend the CKD-EPI equation, and most major laboratories have adopted it for estimated GFR reporting.[2] Creatinine sits on standard metabolic panels, so an eGFR value usually comes along with it. Anyone who keeps their lab reports ends up with a series of eGFR values over the years without ever ordering a kidney test.

That series pairs naturally with the two other markers on the kidney portion of a panel, creatinine and BUN. All three describe the same filtering system from different angles.

What the Research Shows

Estimated GFR has a measured counterpart. In research and confirmatory testing, scientists measure GFR directly by tracking how the kidneys clear an infused marker, and that measured GFR remains the reference standard.[4] Clinical practice guidelines still recommend eGFR for the initial evaluation because it needs only a routine blood draw, and both versions carry some error compared with true GFR.[4]

The estimate leans on assumptions about creatinine itself. Creatinine production depends on muscle mass, which is why measured values differ between males and females and run lower in people with less muscle.[2] Age and sex sit in the equation to account for part of that.[2]

The equation is also newer than it looks. The 2021 CKD-EPI refit, developed and validated in cohorts totaling several thousand participants, replaced earlier versions that included a race coefficient.[3]

Frequently Asked Questions

Educational information based on published sources. Not medical advice. For personal concerns, please consult your doctor.

Is eGFR measured directly?

No. The "e" stands for estimated. A lab measures creatinine in your blood, then a standard equation combines that value with your age and sex to estimate the filtration rate. Directly measured GFR uses an infused tracer and mostly appears in research or as a confirmatory test.

Why does my lab report include eGFR when I didn't order a kidney test?

Most large labs compute eGFR automatically whenever a panel measures creatinine, using the equation recommended by the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology. Because creatinine sits on standard metabolic panels, eGFR usually comes along with it at no extra step.
