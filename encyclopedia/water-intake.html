Water Intake

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Water intake is the volume of fluid, from drinks and water-rich foods, that a person logs over a day. It's a dietary total rather than a lab value or a sensor reading, and the body's actual hydration status is separately regulated through thirst and the kidneys, not read directly from an intake number.[3]

Educational content, not medical advice. For personal or fertility concerns, please consult your doctor.

What Water Intake Measures

Water intake is a running total, not a physiological reading. It adds up what you drink and, when a food log includes it, the water content of what you eat. That's a different kind of number from something like serum sodium, which reflects a moment-in-time concentration in your blood. Water intake only tells you what went in, not how your body is currently managing fluid.

The body's own regulation of that fluid runs through two connected systems: thirst, which is triggered by rising blood osmolality or falling blood volume, and the kidneys, which adjust how much water gets reabsorbed versus excreted largely through the hormone vasopressin (also called antidiuretic hormone). Together these keep total body water within a fairly narrow range regardless of exactly how much someone drinks on a given day.[3]

How It's Measured

There's no sensor for this. Water intake is logged the same way any other food or drink is, either entered manually or synced from a connected app through Apple HealthKit as a dietary water value, and totaled per day like any other tracked nutrient.

Why People Track Water Intake

The most familiar hydration rule, drink eight glasses of water a day, has a shakier research foundation than its popularity suggests. A frequently cited 2002 review in the American Journal of Physiology went looking for the science behind "8 x 8" and didn't find a supporting study. The author traced the number back to a 1945 recommendation for roughly one milliliter of water per calorie of food, a figure that already assumed most of that water would come from food itself, a detail that seems to have gotten dropped somewhere along the way. Published surveys of typical fluid intake also showed plenty of healthy people drinking well under the 8 x 8 amount without documented harm.[1]

There's also a cycle-specific angle to fluid balance. Progesterone rises through the luteal phase, and a 2006 study found progesterone directly correlated with both basal aldosterone and the aldosterone response to a standard hormonal stimulus, a relationship estrogen didn't show.[2] Since aldosterone is the hormone that governs how much sodium, and by extension fluid, the kidneys hold onto, that's a plausible mechanism connecting cycle phase to how the body manages fluid, separate from how much water someone happens to drink that day.

What the Research Shows

The Valtin review remains the standard reference on the eight-glasses claim specifically because it did what the original guideline never did: it went looking for the underlying studies and reported that it couldn't find them. The review does note that higher fluid intake has a real place in specific situations, like vigorous exercise or hot climates, but that's a different, narrower claim than a universal daily number for everyone.[1]

On the hormonal side, the same 2006 study that connected progesterone to aldosterone across the menstrual cycle measured the relationship directly using infused angiotensin II as a standard stimulus, and found progesterone tracked with the aldosterone response independent of the renin-angiotensin system that normally drives it.[2] Basic water-balance physiology describes thirst and vasopressin as the two levers the body actually uses to hold hydration steady day to day, which is a separate, tightly regulated system from whatever number shows up in a food log.[3]

Is there a scientifically proven daily water target, like eight glasses a day?

A widely cited 2002 review looked for research behind the popular eight-glasses-a-day rule and found none. The author traced the number to a 1945 recommendation that already included water from food, a detail that appears to have gotten lost over time, and concluded there was no scientific proof that every healthy person needs that specific amount.

Does Go Go Gaia tell me how much water to drink?

No. Go Go Gaia logs the water intake you enter or that syncs in from Apple Health, and charts your own total over time. It doesn't set a daily target or tell you that you need to drink more or less.

Does fluid balance change across the menstrual cycle?

There's a hormonal mechanism that plausibly connects the two. Progesterone rises through the luteal phase, and a 2006 study found progesterone directly correlated with aldosterone, the hormone the kidneys use to control how much sodium and fluid the body holds onto, a relationship estrogen didn't show.
