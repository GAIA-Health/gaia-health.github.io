Vitamin E

Nutrition & Diet · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Vitamin E, or tocopherol, is a fat-soluble vitamin that works as an antioxidant, protecting cell membranes from damage.[1] The body can't make it. It comes only from plants, formed during photosynthesis.[1][2] Vitamin E exists in eight chemical forms, but only alpha-tocopherol is maintained at meaningful levels in human plasma.[1] Go Go Gaia's food logger tracks it from the meals you log.

What Vitamin E Does

Vitamin E is a fat-soluble vitamin that functions as an antioxidant, protecting the cell membrane from damage.[1] The body doesn't make it. Vitamin E comes solely from the photosynthetic processes of plants, which is why it has to come through diet rather than internal production.[1][2] The mechanism behind that protection is alpha-tocopherol acting as a scavenger of free radicals.[1]

Vitamin E isn't one molecule. It encompasses eight distinct forms: alpha, beta, gamma, and delta-tocopherol, and alpha, beta, gamma, and delta-tocotrienol.[1] Of those eight, only alpha-tocopherol is found in abundance and maintained well in human plasma, which is why it's the form most nutrition research and food-composition data focus on.[1]

Why People Track Vitamin E

Logging it over time, next to other nutrients pulled from the same meals, builds a fuller picture of dietary intake than any single day's totals can on their own.

What the Research Shows

Vitamin E's core identity is well established: it's a fat-soluble antioxidant that protects the cell membrane, formed only by plants during photosynthesis and never produced by the human body.[1]

A separate StatPearls review of vitamin E independently describes it as "a major lipid-soluble antioxidant obtained exclusively from the diet," consistent with the mechanism described above.[2]

Research on vitamin E's chemistry has also clarified that despite eight distinct forms existing in nature, only alpha-tocopherol reaches meaningful, well-maintained concentrations in human plasma, which is why it's treated as the reference form for the vitamin in food-composition data.[1]

Does Go Go Gaia run vitamin E blood tests?

No. Go Go Gaia doesn't offer a lab test for vitamin E. The app's food logger tracks vitamin E from the meals you log, based on their nutrient content, and charts your intake over time.

What's the difference between the eight forms of vitamin E?

Vitamin E includes eight related compounds: alpha, beta, gamma, and delta-tocopherol, and alpha, beta, gamma, and delta-tocotrienol. Only alpha-tocopherol reaches meaningful, well-maintained concentrations in human blood, which is why it's the form most commonly discussed and tracked.

Where does dietary vitamin E come from?

Vitamin E is obtained exclusively from the diet. The human body can't produce it. Olive and sunflower oils, nuts, soybeans, avocados, wheat, and green leafy vegetables are foods rich in it.
