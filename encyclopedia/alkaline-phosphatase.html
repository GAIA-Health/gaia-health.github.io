Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)

Labs & Biomarkers · Reviewed August 2026

Definition

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is an enzyme made in several tissues, mainly the liver and bone, with smaller amounts from the intestine and, during pregnancy, the placenta.[1] A blood ALP result reflects the combined output of those sources. It's measured with a standard blood draw, usually as part of a liver or metabolic panel.[2]

What ALP Measures

ALP isn't a single-organ marker. The enzyme is made in the placenta, intestine, kidney, bone, and liver, and more than 80% of what circulates in blood comes from the liver and bone.[1] A total ALP result is the sum of all those sources at once, which is what makes it different from liver-cell enzymes like ALT and AST.

Each tissue makes its own version of the enzyme, called an isoenzyme. Labs can separate these forms to work out which tissue a given portion of the total came from, though that's a specialized follow-up test rather than part of a routine panel.[1],[3]

How It's Measured

ALP is measured from a standard venous blood draw and reported in units per liter (U/L). It's a routine part of liver panels and the comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP), so most people get an ALP result at a regular checkup without ordering it separately.[2],[4] Labs report their own reference interval with each result, and that interval varies by lab, age, and testing method.

Why People Track ALP

ALP has a women's-health angle that most liver-panel values don't: pregnancy. The placenta makes its own ALP isoenzyme, and in the late third trimester that placental output adds enough to raise total blood ALP as a normal part of pregnancy.[1] A study of healthy pregnant women found the placental form contributing a growing share of total ALP as pregnancy neared term.[5] So the same lab value can read differently before, during, and after a pregnancy, which is exactly the kind of context a dated timeline preserves.

Age matters too. ALP runs naturally higher in childhood and adolescence because growing bone produces the enzyme.[1],[2] Both patterns are population-level physiology, not something to read into any single result.

What the Research Shows

Human ALP exists as several genetically distinct forms, including placental, intestinal, germ-cell, and a tissue-nonspecific type expressed in liver, bone, and kidney.[3]

In healthy pregnant women, the mix of ALP isoenzymes in blood shifts with gestational age. Bone ALP contributed a larger share around weeks 31 to 32, and the placental form contributed relatively more by week 38.[5]

Across the population, ALP levels peak during childhood and puberty, when active bone growth adds bone-derived enzyme to the circulating total.[1]

Related Terms

ALT

A liver-cell enzyme reported next to ALP on liver panels.

AST

Another routine liver-panel enzyme, found in liver, muscle, and other tissues.

Bilirubin

The pigment measured alongside ALP, ALT, and AST on a liver panel.

hCG

Another marker made by the placenta and tracked through pregnancy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does pregnancy change alkaline phosphatase?

The placenta makes its own form of the enzyme, called placental ALP. As pregnancy progresses, that placental output adds to the liver and bone sources already in the blood, so total ALP rises as a normal part of pregnancy rather than a sign that anything changed in the liver or bones.

Is ALP the same as ALT and AST?

No. All three are enzymes that appear together on liver panels, but they're different molecules from different places. ALT and AST sit inside liver cells (AST also in muscle and other tissues), while ALP comes from several tissues at once, mainly the liver's bile-duct lining and bone.
