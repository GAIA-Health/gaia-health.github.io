How caffeine decay works

Caffeine leaves your system on a curve, not a cliff. This calculator uses the exponential decay formula common in pharmacokinetics: remaining amount = dose × 0.5^(hours elapsed ÷ half-life). Every time a half-life passes, whatever caffeine is left gets cut in half again, so the amount never quite hits zero, it just keeps getting smaller.

What half-life actually means here

A half-life of 5 hours means that 5 hours after your coffee, about half the caffeine is still circulating; 10 hours in, about a quarter is left; 15 hours in, about an eighth. The textbook average sits around 5 hours, but the real range runs roughly 4 to 7 hours depending on genetics, liver enzyme activity, and other factors, which is why this calculator asks for a metabolism speed instead of assuming one number for everyone.

Where the 50 mg cutoff comes from

There's no universal "safe" cutoff dose, since sensitivity to caffeine at bedtime varies by person. This calculator uses 50 mg circulating at bedtime as a reference point, roughly half a cup of coffee's worth, and solves backward from your bedtime to find the latest time you could have that specific drink and still land under it. Raise or lower your own bar by adjusting bedtime or trying a smaller serving and re-running the numbers.

Your cycle and caffeine

The birth control checkbox above only applies to combined hormonal methods, the ones that include estrogen: the combined pill, the patch, and the ring. Progestin-only methods, like the mini-pill or a hormonal IUD, don't carry the same estrogen-driven slowdown in caffeine clearance, so this calculator's 1.9x multiplier isn't meant for those. If you're not sure which type you're on, our guide to birth control methods breaks down what's in each one.

The same logic extends to a natural cycle: estrogen rises and falls across the month, so caffeine tends to clear a bit slower when estrogen is higher (roughly the luteal phase) and a bit faster when it's lower. That shift is real but smaller than the birth control effect, which is why this calculator doesn't build a separate multiplier for cycle phase the way it does for birth control, it's more of a pattern worth tracking than a number worth calculating.

Common Questions About Caffeine Timing

Caffeine has an average half-life of about 5 hours, meaning half of what you drank is still circulating 5 hours later, and a quarter is still there after 10 hours. It doesn't disappear all at once. A 95 mg cup of coffee at 2 PM still has roughly 24 mg left in your system at midnight. That's why an afternoon coffee can quietly affect a bedtime that feels hours away.

Half-life is the time it takes your body to clear half the caffeine you took in. The textbook average is around 5 hours, but it ranges roughly 4 to 7 hours depending on genetics, liver enzyme activity, smoking status, and what else you've eaten or taken. This calculator lets you pick fast, average, or slow to reflect that range, since there's no single number that fits everyone.

Tolerance changes how caffeine feels, not how fast it leaves. Regular caffeine use can blunt the jittery or alert feeling from a given dose, but the liver enzyme that clears caffeine doesn't work any faster just because you drink it every day. That's why someone who feels nothing from an afternoon coffee can still have measurable caffeine circulating at bedtime, tolerance changes the subjective effect, not the half-life this calculator uses.

It depends on how much caffeine you had, your metabolism speed, and what time you're winding down, so there isn't one blanket answer. This calculator solves it backward from your own bedtime, drink, and metabolism speed instead. As a reference point, a 5-hour half-life means caffeine taken 8 to 9 hours before bed is usually mostly cleared, but mostly still isn't zero.

Decaf isn't caffeine-free. It typically runs about 2 to 15 mg per cup versus roughly 95 mg in regular drip coffee. At that dose, decay time barely matters since it starts out close to the threshold this calculator uses. For most people, the bigger sleep-timing factor is regular coffee, tea, energy drinks, or soda earlier in the day, not a decaf in the evening.
