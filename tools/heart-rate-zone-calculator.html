Why 220 minus age is wrong for women

The formula most people know, 220 minus age, comes from a 1971 review of earlier studies that were overwhelmingly conducted on men. It became the default because it's simple, not because it was validated on women. In 2010, a study by Gulati and colleagues measured maximal exercise heart rate directly in a large cohort of asymptomatic women and produced a different formula: 206 minus 0.88 times age. For most adult ages, that lands lower than 220 minus age, which means the older formula tends to overestimate a woman's true max heart rate. Every zone calculated from an inflated max heart rate ends up inflated too, so your "moderate" effort might actually be your "hard" one.

What the five zones mean

Heart rate zones split effort into five bands, each defined as a percentage of your max heart rate (or, with the Karvonen method, a percentage of your heart rate reserve added to your resting heart rate). Zone 1 is a warm-up pace, zone 2 is the easy, sustainable pace that makes up the bulk of most endurance training, zone 3 is a moderate "tempo" effort, zone 4 is hard, and zone 5 is close to an all-out sprint.

Zone 2, specifically

Zone 2 (roughly 60 to 70% of max heart rate, or 60 to 70% of heart rate reserve with Karvonen) is an easy, conversational effort. You should be able to talk in full sentences without gasping, even if you'd rather not try to sing. It's the zone that shows up most often in endurance training research as the foundation of a training week, with harder efforts layered on top rather than replacing it. It's also the zone most people run too fast, because "easy" doesn't feel like it's doing anything.

Percent of max vs. Karvonen

The plain percent-of-max method just multiplies your estimated max heart rate by the zone percentage. The Karvonen formula uses heart rate reserve (HRR) instead: HRR = max heart rate − resting heart rate, and each zone's target is resting heart rate + (percentage × HRR). Because Karvonen factors in your resting heart rate, it produces zones that are more personalized to your own cardiovascular baseline than a straight percentage of a population-average max.

The Gulati formula estimates maximum heart rate for women as 206 minus 0.88 times age. It comes from a 2010 study (Gulati et al.) that measured maximal exercise heart rate in a large cohort of asymptomatic women, rather than extrapolating from the mostly-male data behind 220 minus age. It's still a population average and not a substitute for a measured max, but it's the better starting formula for women.

The Karvonen formula calculates training zones from heart rate reserve (HRR) instead of max heart rate alone. HRR is your max heart rate minus your resting heart rate. Each zone's bpm target is resting heart rate plus a percentage of HRR, for example zone 2 at 60 to 70% would be resting HR + 0.6×HRR to resting HR + 0.7×HRR. Resting and submaximal heart rate tend to run a few beats per minute higher in the luteal phase (after ovulation, before your period) than in the follicular phase, tracking the rise in progesterone and core body temperature. That means the same pace or effort can land in a slightly higher heart rate zone in the luteal phase than it would earlier in your cycle, even though the effort itself hasn't changed.

Estimate your max heart rate with the Gulati formula (206 minus 0.88 times age), then take 60 to 70% of that number for a plain percent-of-max zone 2. If you know your resting heart rate, the Karvonen method sharpens it further: subtract resting heart rate from max heart rate to get heart rate reserve, then zone 2 is resting heart rate plus 60 to 70% of that reserve. A watch that logs your actual resting heart rate over time will generally track closer to your real physiology than either formula alone.
