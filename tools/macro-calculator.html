Calories, macros & your cycle

This calculator starts with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, one of the more accurate published formulas for estimating basal metabolic rate (BMR) from weight, height, age, and sex: 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age − 161 for women. Multiplying BMR by an activity factor (1.2 to 1.725, depending on how much you move) gives total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE, the number most people mean when they ask how many calories they need. A goal adjustment then nudges that up or down: roughly −15% to support fat loss, +10% to support muscle building, or no change at all to maintain.

Why one number isn't the whole story

Almost every calorie calculator online stops there, with a single daily number. Research on resting energy expenditure across the menstrual cycle finds it runs roughly 2 to 9 percent higher in the luteal phase, the one to two weeks after ovulation, than in the follicular phase before it. That's a real, measured shift in how much energy your body is using at rest, not a story about willpower or discipline. This calculator shows both bands side by side so a bigger number in the back half of your cycle reads as expected, not as a lapse.

How the macro split works

Protein uses the same published gram-per-kilogram ranges as our protein calculator, from about 1.2 g/kg for a mostly sedentary day up to 2.0 g/kg for resistance training or a fat-loss phase, with perimenopause and postmenopause nudged toward the higher end on the anabolic-resistance rationale. Fat is set at 0.8 to 1.0 g/kg. Carbohydrate fills whatever calories are left, which is also why carbohydrate is the macro that moves most between your follicular and luteal bands: protein and fat targets are tied to your bodyweight and stay fairly steady, while the extra calories in the luteal phase show up mostly as more carbohydrate.

These are estimates, and tracking them is optional

Every number on this page comes from population formulas applied to what you typed in. They're a reasonable starting point, not a personalized prescription, and they can be off in either direction for your actual metabolism. It's also worth saying plainly: if counting grams and calories tends to make food feel harder rather than easier, not tracking them is a legitimate choice, not a failure to optimize.

BMR (basal metabolic rate) is the energy your body uses at complete rest, just to keep your organs running. TDEE (total daily energy expenditure) is BMR multiplied by an activity factor to account for movement, exercise, and daily life on top of that resting baseline. TDEE is the number most people mean when they ask "how many calories do I need," since it reflects an actual day rather than a body lying still.

This calculator uses the same published gram-per-kilogram protein ranges as our protein calculator (roughly 1.2 g/kg for sedentary up to 2.0 g/kg for resistance training or a fat-loss phase), fat at 0.8 to 1.0 g/kg, and fills the remaining calories with carbohydrate. Protein and fat targets are based on your bodyweight and stay fairly constant; carbohydrate is what flexes to cover the difference between your follicular and luteal calorie bands.

In the luteal phase, the one to two weeks before your period, resting energy expenditure runs modestly higher and appetite commonly rises alongside it, both linked to the hormonal shifts after ovulation. Eating somewhat more during that window lines up with what your body is actually doing, rather than working against it. This calculator's luteal band reflects that shift; it isn't a suggestion to under-eat in the follicular phase to compensate.
