Creatine and the female-physiology gap

Creatine is one of the most studied supplements around, but most of that history runs through men. Early dosing research, the flat "5 g a day" shorthand, and a lot of the loading-phase literature were built on studies of male athletes. Women were largely left out, which matters here because it's part of why bodyweight-scaled dosing, rather than one flat scoop, tracks closer to what a smaller or larger body actually needs.

Why bodyweight matters more than a flat number

Women tend to store roughly 70 to 80% of the creatine that men do at baseline, largely tied to differences in muscle mass. A flat 5 g dose sized around an average male bodyweight can be more, or less, than the g/kg protocol figures below actually call for once you scale to your own weight. That's the whole reason this calculator asks for your weight instead of quoting one number for everyone.

Beyond muscle: what the newer research is asking

The emerging research interest in creatine for women isn't only about muscle and strength. Researchers are also looking at cognition, mood, bone density, and recovery, particularly around perimenopause and postmenopause, when estrogen decline affects several of those same systems. This is a genuinely active research area, and it's still early. It's worth knowing it exists, not treating it as settled.

Creatine across your cycle: early, not established

There is early work looking at creatine metabolism across the menstrual cycle and through the menopause transition, but the evidence here is genuinely thin. It does not currently support taking more or less at a given point in your cycle, and no calculator, including this one, can tell you a phase-specific dose. What's being studied is worth knowing about. It isn't yet something to act on.

Common Questions About Creatine & Dosing

The maintenance dose used in most of the research is about 0.1 grams per kilogram of bodyweight per day, so a 68 kg (150 lb) woman lands around 6.8 g/day. That is close to why a flat 5 g scoop became the popular shorthand, but scaling it to your own weight gets closer to what the studies actually tested. This is the protocol used in the research, not a personalized prescription, and your own response is worth paying attention to over time.

A loading phase is a short stretch, typically 5 to 7 days, at a higher dose (around 0.3 g/kg/day, usually split into 4 smaller doses through the day) before dropping to the regular maintenance amount. It is not required. Going straight to the maintenance dose gets muscle creatine stores to roughly the same place, it just takes longer. Loading is a matter of how fast you get there, not a different destination.

With a loading phase, muscle creatine stores are generally reported to reach saturation in about a week. Skipping loading and going straight to the maintenance dose gets to roughly the same point, just slower, generally cited at around 3 to 4 weeks of daily use. Either estimate describes the protocol used in published research, not a guaranteed timeline for any one person.

Most of the foundational creatine dosing research was conducted in men, and a lot of general guidance still defaults to a flat 5 g scoop that was sized for an average male bodyweight. Women tend to store roughly 70 to 80% of the creatine that men do at baseline, which is part of why scaling the dose to your own bodyweight, rather than using one flat number, tracks closer to what the underlying research actually measured.

This is an area of early, emerging research rather than an established finding. Interest in creatine for women has expanded beyond muscle to include cognition, mood, bone, and recovery, and there is early work looking at creatine across the menstrual cycle and through perimenopause and postmenopause. None of it currently supports adjusting your dose for a particular cycle phase, and this calculator does not attempt to estimate one.
